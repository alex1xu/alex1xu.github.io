<SeriesNav dir="prev" href="/blog/mario-markets" kicker="Part 1" title="Rebuilding a trading exchange for speed and correctness" />

[The first post](/blog/mario-markets) rebuilt a prediction-market exchange around a single-writer Rust engine. The engine matched a command in about 2 microseconds and had substantial headroom. The full system, however, initially saturated near 20,000 orders per second while delivering acknowledgements, fills, and market data to 250 clients.

<Callout type="lesson" title="TL;DR">
I raised the full system's ceiling from 20k to 97k orders per second through six rounds of measurement, changes, and load testing. Each round exposed a different bottleneck.

- Three fixes barely moved throughput: taking a pegged routing thread off the async runtime, removing the allocation the profiler ranked highest, and capping market-data snapshots to ten price levels. Each reduced a real cost, but none was the throughput constraint.
- Batching per-trade broadcasts into 20 Hz ticks only nudged the knee ~27k→~31k, but mattered most for removing an eviction cliff.
- Removing an `fsync` that gated order admission was a major fix (~31k→~54k). It was off-CPU disk work, so a CPU profiler did not rank it. Sharding a single delivery thread across cores was the other major fix (~54k→~97k).
- At one point the load generators burned more CPU parsing market data than the server spent producing it and capped out at ~80k orders a second.
</Callout>

## The delivery cost wasn't where part one guessed

Part one left two open questions: was the ~20k ceiling really the server, and where did delivery become expensive? I expected per-client message re-encoding to dominate, but it was only 0.4% of CPU. The real firehose was market data. Every trade became a separate frame sent to 250 receivers. The broadcast machinery and its shared-buffer reference counting used about a fifth of CPU—about a third including the socket writes they triggered. The dropped-frame counter reached 6 million in 15 seconds, about 13 drops per order.

<ProfilePanel stage="fanout" />

The re-encode wasn't the cost because order acks are low-volume next to the trade firehose. The encode-once work from part one hadn't failed. It had just removed the serde layer that was masking the broadcast machinery beneath it.

Delivery has two lanes with different contracts. The reliable lane is per client and sequence-numbered: losing an acknowledgement or fill is a bug, so an overloaded client is disconnected instead of silently losing a message. The market-data lane carries latest-only snapshots. Dropping one is safe because the next 20 Hz snapshot replaces it.

Trades use the latest-only lane, so coalescing them into one frame per 20 Hz tick drops nothing clients need to reconstruct the current market. Dropped frames went from about 1 million per run to zero, and in-system p99 at 30k fell from 4.2 ms to 262 µs. The slow-client eviction cliff disappeared. Sustained throughput moved only from about 27k to 31k, then held flat across 40k, 50k, and 60k offered while half the machine was idle. That pointed to one serial stage, not total CPU exhaustion.

## The pegged thread was mostly runtime overhead

After the batching fix, I re-profiled and found one worker still pegged while six idled. That worker was the routing consumer, the single task that drains the engine's output and hands each event to the right client. The natural read is that this stage is expensive and should be made cheaper. But when I broke down its core, the tokio runtime (scheduler, `clock_gettime`, epoll, timer wheel) came to most of it, waking the 250 downstream tasks another ~9%, and the actual routing work only ~8%. About 92% of the pegged core was async-runtime overhead rather than the routing the thread existed to do.

<ProfilePanel stage="runtime" />

The routing consumer was an async task that was almost always ready because another event was already waiting. It therefore kept using scheduler machinery designed for tasks that spend time waiting on I/O. I moved it to a dedicated busy-poll thread, like the engine. Its CPU use fell from 99% to about 30%, and machine idle rose from about 52% to 65%. The change removed real overhead and freed a core, but throughput did not move: that core had not been the limiting resource.

## An `fsync` gating admission — invisible to the profiler

With the routing thread quiet, the profiler produced a new headline: the engine was pegged at 99.9%, apparently engine-bound. But actual matching was only about 2.5% of samples. The rest was the engine's busy-spin loop reading an empty input ring. It was not busy matching; it was starved of commands. That moved the search upstream, into the admission path. A CPU profiler could not point there because the culprit was not consuming CPU. The journal thread was blocked in disk `fsync`, waiting in the kernel. Sampling profilers mostly attribute on-CPU time, so a thread parked on disk I/O barely appears.

<ProfilePanel stage="journal" />

<JournalCoupling />

The bug was the order of handoff. Ingress sent each command toward the journal before it handed it to the engine, through a bounded queue. When the disk fell behind, that queue filled; admission blocked; and the engine starved. The design explicitly allowed a small durability window for this live event, so a slow journal was never supposed to block matching. The fix was to restore the intended tee: hand the command to the engine immediately and independently send it to the journal.

<Callout type="warning" title="The fsync I mismeasured by 6000×">
Before trusting that `fsync` was the wall, I benchmarked it on the server's disk. My first attempt rewrote a small fixed file and reported p50 0.3 µs — page-cache-resident, with no real durability barrier, and if I'd stopped there I'd have ruled out the disk and hunted in the wrong place. The journal doesn't rewrite a fixed file. It appends to a growing one, which forces the filesystem to flush new metadata too, and reproducing that pattern gave p50 1.9 ms. That lands where it should: 64 orders per 1.9 ms is ~34,000 a second, the plateau I'd been stuck at. The append-benchmark rate, the measured plateau, and the journal-lag counter pinned at its channel cap all pointed at the same ~34k. I'd been benchmarking a convenient proxy instead of the thing as it actually runs.
</Callout>

The ceiling roughly doubled, from about 31k to 54k orders/s, with no acknowledgement lost or reordered at any tested rate. It was the first change that materially moved the number, and it addressed work that a CPU flamegraph could not rank because the cost was off-CPU.

<Callout type="lesson" title="A green test suite proved the wrong thing">
This fix carried a scar worth more than the win. Enlarging the durability batch to make the disk more efficient silently widened the durability window. The journal flushed on an idle-gap timer that reset on every arrival, which is fine when orders are sparse, but under sustained load the timer never fires, so the only remaining flush trigger became the batch count, and that stretched a few-millisecond window into hundreds of milliseconds of acknowledged-but-unflushed fills. My full suite stayed green, because it proved determinism — replay the log, get the identical round — which says nothing about how long a fill sits unflushed. A reviewer caught it by reasoning about the mechanism, not from a failing test. A performance knob had been coupled to a correctness property through a third mechanism, and nothing was testing that seam.
</Callout>

## The engine looked bound again — the same busy-spin trap

At the new knee, the profile again put the engine at 99.9% CPU. Again, that did not mean matching was the limit. In-system p50 was only 10 µs. The engine was waiting on a full output ring for its slowest consumer to advance, and busy-spinning while it waited.

The real cost sat three rows down. That consumer was cloning each message into a per-client replay buffer under a lock, 14.7% memmove plus 5.6% allocator, while the threads that actually write to sockets sat idle.

<ProfilePanel stage="clone" />

I put messages behind Rust's standard atomic reference-counting pointer, `Arc`, so buffering, broadcasting, and encoding could share one message instead of deep-copying it per event. A pointer-equality test verified the path was clone-free. The knee moved from 53,995 to 53,987 orders/s—eight acknowledgements, well inside measurement noise.

Another real optimization, another flat knee. The decisive clue was the latency split, not the profile. In-system p50 was 10 µs, while client-observed “network” time was 16.8 ms—about 1,700× larger—and the server was 55% idle. The bottleneck was in delivery or input handling, not total CPU capacity.

## Localizing the wall with `ss`, not `perf`

Two null results from guessing was enough. Before changing another line, I wanted to see where the queue was actually building — the server failing to push market data out, or the load generators failing to read it in. Those need opposite fixes, so instead of guessing a third time I read socket-queue depth on every box.

<TwoSidedQueue />

The server's inbound socket queue held 126 MB of orders it had not read. Its outbound queue held only 43 KB, and both load generators were near zero. The clients were reading normally. The server was failing to read its own inbound socket because the downstream delivery path was saturated. Once the routing consumer cannot drain quickly enough, the engine eventually runs out of output-ring space, input stops being read, and the kernel's inbound queue grows.

That was also the "network" latency. In-system time gets stamped only after a task reads a frame off the socket, so an order's wait in that inbound queue falls outside the measurement and gets blamed on the wire. The 16.8 ms and the backlog were the same thing.

## The rig was the limiter again — the load generators

To push the server past ~54k I had to offer it more than ~54k, and at those rates the load generators became the ceiling. Each burned ~700% CPU and pegged its box above ~80k offered, so a run up there was measuring the generator, not the server — the same trap as part one's single load box, one layer up.

The bottleneck in the generators was their read path. Every simulated client fully parsed every frame, including a 99-level book snapshot sent 20 times per second to each of 250 clients. The benchmark did not need that payload. It only recorded acknowledgements, fills, rejections, and a few sequence-gap counters. The fix was to read a small header from every frame—enough to identify its type and sequence number—and fully parse only the acknowledgement, fill, and rejection frames used by the metrics.

The distinction matters: parse less, not read less. The generator still drains every socket frame. If clients stopped reading, server egress would back up and the benchmark would trigger the slow-client eviction it was meant to measure. Skipping unnecessary parsing after a frame is read costs the server nothing. With that change and four load boxes instead of two, the generators were no longer the ceiling. The system reached about 85k with the old generators still co-limiting the run, and a clean 97k once the measurement rig was fixed.

## Sharding the delivery path — the second doubling

The wall was structural. One thread drained the engine's output and routed every event to 250 client lanes. Delivery therefore remained serial, no matter how idle the other cores were. The question was how to split it across cores without reintroducing shared per-client state.

<ShardingTopology />

I split clients across four shards using `user_id % 4`. Each shard is one thread that owns its clients end to end: it reads the engine output from its own cursor, routes only events for its roughly 62 clients, and owns those clients' delivery lanes. No two shards touch the same client, so there is no shared per-client state to protect. The output ring already supported independent consumers for market data, so the change was structurally small: request four delivery cursors instead of one, then let each shard do about a quarter of the routing work.

<Callout type="note" title="Why hash by client">
Two other splits were tempting. Sharding by market splits the wrong thing: delivery is the bottleneck, and a client trades across all four markets, so it would scatter one client's event stream across shards and complicate ordering. A work-stealing pool balances load more evenly, but lets any worker touch any client's state. Hashing by client keeps a client's whole stream on one thread, so its ordering follows naturally. The tradeoff remains: one shard with many slow clients can still slow the engine. Sharding reduces that risk; it does not eliminate it.
</Callout>

The correctness story remained simple. A client stays on one shard for its whole session, so its sequence numbers remain gap-free. Each shard keeps its own leaderboard copy; that is safe because every shard observes the same deterministic event stream and computes the same result. The one non-idempotent settlement action—writing the results archive—runs only on shard 0. A differential test feeds two shards the same stream and checks that each delivers only its own clients' acknowledgements. With one shard, the topology reduces to the original single-consumer version, so the earlier tests still apply.

With four shards and the fixed load generators, the clean server knee was ~97k orders/s, up from ~54k, with `reliable_gaps=0` at every rate including deep overload. The profile at that knee shows the single pegged consumer is gone:

<ShardedSplit />

Where one thread had held a whole core routing everything, the work now spreads evenly across the four shards, within 3% of each other. The machine is busy everywhere instead of stuck on one stage. The largest cost left is copying frame bytes out to the 250 connections — `memset` plus `memmove`, ~11.5% of samples.

## Capping the snapshot bytes — a third null result

That made frame-copying the obvious next target. If copying bytes to clients costs time, smaller snapshots should help. I capped each snapshot to the ten price levels nearest the spread—the depth the client actually renders—down from about 49 per side. The change was correct, but the knee moved only from 97.3k to 97.7k.

The reason is frame count, not snapshot size. At the knee, the market-data lane sends about 5,000 snapshots per second, while the reliable lane sends about 97,000 acknowledgements—one per order. Snapshots were a small fraction of copied bytes and frames, so shrinking them barely changed total work. The profiler had identified a real cost, just not the dominant cost. Fewer reliable frames per client, not smaller snapshots, would be the next meaningful lever.

## What the hunt taught me

The ceiling went from ~31k to ~54k once the journal stopped gating admission, then to ~97k once delivery was sharded across cores, with correctness holding at every step.

Two lessons stuck with me, both about not trusting the obvious signal.

- **You cannot predict the next bottleneck from the current one.** The constraints were, in turn, a market-data firehose, async-runtime overhead, an I/O-coupling bug, copying under a lock, a serial delivery stage, and frame count rather than frame size. Each fix revealed a different limit.
- **The profiler's hottest line is not necessarily the constraint.** Three changes targeted the top flamegraph cost and barely moved the knee. A busy-spinning thread at 100% only proves it is spinning. The two biggest wins came from what CPU sampling could not rank: an `fsync` waiting off-CPU and a serial stage localized through socket-queue measurements with `ss`.

The engine from part one still hasn't broken a sweat. Everything hard about making this exchange fast was the fan-out from one writer to many readers.
