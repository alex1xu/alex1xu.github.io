<SeriesNav dir="prev" href="/blog/mario-markets" kicker="Part 1" title="Rebuilding a trading exchange for speed and correctness" />

[The first post](/blog/mario-markets) rebuilt a prediction-market exchange into a single-writer, lock-free Rust engine. The engine matched an order in about 2 microseconds and had enormous headroom, but the whole system saturated near 20,000 orders a second delivering fills and market data back out to 250 clients.

<Callout type="lesson" title="TL;DR">
I raised my exchange's ceiling from 20k to 97k orders per second through six rounds of profiling, optimizing, and load-testing. Each round was a different bottleneck.

- Three fixes moved throughput by nothing: taking a pegged routing thread off the async runtime, removing the allocation the profiler ranked highest, and capping the market-data snapshot to ten price levels. Each did exactly what it was designed to, but the knee held. I learned that the profiler's hottest item isn't always the actual constraint.
- Batching per-trade broadcasts into 20 Hz ticks only nudged the knee ~27k→~31k, but mattered most for removing an eviction cliff.
- Removing an `fsync` gating order admission, which is off-CPU work the profiler didn't rank, was a major fix (~31k→~54k). Sharding the single delivery thread across cores was also major (~54k→~97k).
- At one point the load generators burned more CPU parsing market data than the server spent producing it and capped out at ~80k orders a second.
</Callout>

## The delivery cost wasn't what part one guessed

In part one I wasn't sure how much of the ~20k ceiling was the server versus my single load box, and where on the delivery path the cost sat. So I profiled, expecting the per-client message re-encode I'd flagged in part one to be the bottleneck — but that path only came out at 0.4% of CPU. The real firehose was market data. Every trade was broadcast individually to 250 receivers, each written as its own frame, and that broadcast plus the reference-counting on the shared buffers came to about a fifth of CPU, a third once you count the socket writes it drove. The dropped-frame counter showed 6 million in 15 seconds (about 13 drops per order).

<ProfilePanel stage="fanout" />

The re-encode wasn't the cost because order acks are low-volume next to the trade firehose. The encode-once work from part one hadn't failed. It had just removed the serde layer that was masking the broadcast machinery beneath it.

Delivery splits into two lanes with opposite contracts. The reliable lane is per-client and sequence-numbered, a missing ack is a bug, so overload evicts a client rather than dropping its message. The lossy lane is one broadcast of latest-only market data, where a dropped frame self-heals on the next 20 Hz tick.

Trades ride the lossy, latest-only lane, so coalescing them into one frame per 20 Hz tick drops nothing a client is entitled to. Dropped frames went from ~1 million per run to zero, in-system p99 at 30k fell from 4.2 ms to 262 µs, and the eviction cliff vanished. But the sustained knee only crept from ~27k to a ~31k plateau that held flat across 40k, 50k, and 60k offered with half the machine idle — a serialization limit, not CPU exhaustion.

## The pegged thread was mostly runtime overhead

After the batching fix, I re-profiled and found one worker still pegged while six idled. That worker was the routing consumer, the single task that drains the engine's output and hands each event to the right client. The natural read is that this stage is expensive and should be made cheaper. But when I broke down its core, the tokio runtime (scheduler, `clock_gettime`, epoll, timer wheel) came to most of it, waking the 250 downstream tasks another ~9%, and the actual routing work only ~8%. About 92% of the pegged core was async-runtime overhead rather than the routing the thread existed to do.

<ProfilePanel stage="runtime" />

The reason is that the consumer is an async task that's almost always ready — there's almost always an event waiting — so it never yields for lack of work. Each poll runs to the end of its scheduling budget, reschedules itself, and gets re-polled, repaying the full async-task overhead every cycle. So I moved it off the async runtime onto a dedicated busy-poll thread, the way the engine already runs. The thread's CPU dropped from 99% to ~30%, and machine idle rose from ~52% to ~65%. The fix was correct and it freed a whole core — but throughput didn't move.

## An `fsync` gating admission — invisible to the profiler

With the routing thread quiet, the profiler moved to a new headline. The engine was pegged at 99.9%, apparently engine-bound. But the engine's actual matching work was ~2.5% of samples, and the rest was its busy-spin loop reading an empty input ring — it wasn't working, it was starved, spinning on nothing. That put the constraint upstream of the engine, in the path that feeds it orders, which is not where a CPU profile points, because the culprit wasn't burning CPU. It was asleep. The journal thread, which writes every order to a durable log, was blocked in an uninterruptible disk `fsync`, and a sampling profiler counts on-CPU work, so a thread parked waiting for the disk barely registers.

<ProfilePanel stage="journal" />

<JournalCoupling />

The bug was ordering. Ingress wrote each order to the journal before handing it to the engine, over a bounded queue, so when the disk fell behind the queue filled, admission blocked, and the engine starved. My own spec says in three places that a slow journal must never gate the engine (durability is explicitly relaxed, since losing the last few milliseconds on a crash is acceptable for a live event), so this was a bug against the design rather than a tradeoff to weigh. The fix was to do what the spec already required, and hand the order to the engine first, teeing it to the journal in parallel.

<Callout type="warning" title="The fsync I mismeasured by 6000×">
Before trusting that `fsync` was the wall, I benchmarked it on the server's disk. My first attempt rewrote a small fixed file and reported p50 0.3 µs — page-cache-resident, with no real durability barrier, and if I'd stopped there I'd have ruled out the disk and hunted in the wrong place. The journal doesn't rewrite a fixed file. It appends to a growing one, which forces the filesystem to flush new metadata too, and reproducing that pattern gave p50 1.9 ms. That lands where it should: 64 orders per 1.9 ms is ~34,000 a second, the plateau I'd been stuck at. The append-benchmark rate, the measured plateau, and the journal-lag counter pinned at its channel cap all pointed at the same ~34k. I'd been benchmarking a convenient proxy instead of the thing as it actually runs.
</Callout>

The ceiling roughly doubled, from ~31k to ~54k orders/s, with no order ack lost or reordered at any rate. It is the first change in the hunt that moved the number, and it addressed something no flamegraph had ranked, because the cost was off-CPU.

<Callout type="lesson" title="A green test suite proved the wrong thing">
This fix carried a scar worth more than the win. Enlarging the durability batch to make the disk more efficient silently widened the durability window. The journal flushed on an idle-gap timer that reset on every arrival, which is fine when orders are sparse, but under sustained load the timer never fires, so the only remaining flush trigger became the batch count, and that stretched a few-millisecond window into hundreds of milliseconds of acknowledged-but-unflushed fills. My full suite stayed green, because it proved determinism — replay the log, get the identical round — which says nothing about how long a fill sits unflushed. A reviewer caught it by reasoning about the mechanism, not from a failing test. A performance knob had been coupled to a correctness property through a third mechanism, and nothing was testing that seam.
</Callout>

## The engine looked bound again — the same busy-spin trap

At the new knee the profile read the same as before. The engine sat at 99.9%, top symbol by a wide margin, and it was the same busy-spin misread. It was waiting, not working. In-system latency was 10 µs p50, and the rest of the time it spun on a full output ring, waiting for its slowest consumer to take an item.

The real cost sat three rows down. That consumer was cloning each message into a per-client replay buffer under a lock, 14.7% memmove plus 5.6% allocator, while the threads that actually write to sockets sat idle.

<ProfilePanel stage="clone" />

So I made the message reference-counted behind an `Arc`, so buffering, broadcasting, and encoding all share one copy instead of a deep clone per event, and I checked it was clone-free with a pointer-equality test. The knee moved from 53,995 to 53,987 orders/s — eight acks, inside noise.

Another correct fix, another flat knee. The latency split is what finally showed the wall. In-system time was 10 µs p50, while the client-observed "network" time was 16.8 ms p50, about 1700× larger, and the server sat 55% idle the whole time. The bottleneck was somewhere in delivery, and the server wasn't CPU-bound at all.

## Localizing the wall with `ss`

Two null results from guessing, and that was enough. Before changing another line, I wanted to see where the queue was actually building — the server failing to push market data out, or the load generators failing to read it in. Those need opposite fixes, so instead of guessing a third time I read socket-queue depth on every box.

<TwoSidedQueue />

The server's inbound queue held 126 MB of orders it hadn't read yet. Its outbound queue held 43 KB, and both load generators sat near zero. The clients were reading fine. The server couldn't read its own inbound socket, because everything behind it was saturated. When the routing consumer can't drain fast enough, the engine stalls on ring space, stops reading input, and the kernel's inbound queue balloons.

That was also the "network" latency. In-system time gets stamped only after a task reads a frame off the socket, so an order's wait in that inbound queue falls outside the measurement and gets blamed on the wire. The 16.8 ms and the backlog were the same thing.

## The rig was the limiter again — the load generators

To push the server past ~54k I had to offer it more than ~54k, and at those rates the load generators became the ceiling. Each burned ~700% CPU and pegged its box above ~80k offered, so a run up there was measuring the generator, not the server — the same trap as part one's single load box, one layer up.

The cause was on the read side. Every simulated client fully parsed every frame, and the heaviest are the market-data broadcasts, a full 99-level book snapshot sent 20 times a second to each of 250 clients. The load generator doesn't use any of that. It records acks, fills, and rejects, tracks a couple of gap counters, and throws away all the book depth and trade bodies it just parsed. So I stopped parsing them. It reads a slim header off every frame to get the type and sequence number, and only fully parses the ack, fill, and reject frames the metrics need.

The trick is to parse less, not read less. The socket still drains every frame, because if a client stops reading, the server's egress backs up and trips the slow-client eviction I was trying to measure in the first place. But skipping the parse of a frame I've already read costs the server nothing. That, plus moving from two load boxes to four, dropped the generators to 47–85% idle at every rate. This is why the sharding number below comes in two parts. It was ~85k with the old generators still co-limiting the run, and a clean ~97k once the instrument was fixed.

## Sharding the delivery path — the second doubling

The wall was structural. One thread drained the engine's output and routed every event to all 250 clients, so delivery stayed a single serial stage no matter how idle the other cores were. The part-one plan was to split it across cores. The real question was how to do that without putting the shared state back that the single-writer engine existed to avoid.

<ShardingTopology />

I split clients across four shards by `user_id % 4`. Each shard is one thread that owns its clients end to end — reads the engine's output on its own cursor, routes only the events for the ~62 clients it owns, and holds their lanes itself. No two shards ever touch the same client, so there's no shared state to guard. Almost none of this was new: the output ring was already built to feed multiple readers, each on its own cursor, back in part one for the market-data reader. Going from one router to four is just asking it for four cursors instead of one, and each shard does a quarter of the work.

<Callout type="note" title="Why hash by client">
Two other splits were on the table. Sharding by market splits the wrong side — the engine, not delivery — and it scatters one client's stream across shards, since a client trades all four racers at once, which breaks per-client ordering. A work-stealing pool balances load better but lets any worker touch any client, putting the shared state right back that I was trying to get rid of. Hashing by client keeps each client's whole stream on one thread, so its ordering comes for free. The tradeoff is that a shard whose clients are all slow can still stall the engine, the same way the single consumer could. Sharding doesn't remove that case, but with each shard doing a quarter of the work it happens less often.
</Callout>

The correctness held up. Each client's sequence numbers stay gap-free because one shard owns it for the whole session. The leaderboard is copied onto every shard rather than shared, which is safe because every shard sees the same events and computes an identical board. The one thing that can't be duplicated — writing the results archive at settlement — is pinned to shard 0. A differential test drives two shards with the same stream and checks each delivers only its own clients' acks and doesn't touch the other's state. And with one shard the whole thing collapses back to the old single-consumer setup, so the part-one tests still run.

With four shards and the fixed load generators, the clean server knee was ~97k orders/s, up from ~54k, with `reliable_gaps=0` at every rate including deep overload. The profile at that knee shows the single pegged consumer is gone:

<ShardedSplit />

Where one thread had held a whole core routing everything, the work now spreads evenly across the four shards, within 3% of each other. The machine is busy everywhere instead of stuck on one stage. The largest cost left is copying frame bytes out to the 250 connections — `memset` plus `memmove`, ~11.5% of samples.

## Capping the snapshot bytes — a third null result

That memmove was the obvious next target. If the cost is copying frame bytes to clients, sending fewer bytes should help, and the fattest frames are the book snapshots. So I capped each one to the ten price levels nearest the spread — all a client renders anyway — down from ~49 a side. Correct, passed review, moved the knee from 97.3k to 97.7k. Nothing.

The catch is that the copy cost tracks the *number* of frames, not their size. At the knee the market-data lane sends ~5,000 snapshot frames a second, against ~97,000 acks on the reliable lane, one per order. Snapshots were always a small fraction of what gets copied, so shrinking them barely moved total CPU. This time the profiler had pointed at a real cost — the memmove was there — it just wasn't the *dominant* one. Having fewer frames per client, not smaller ones, would actually move this knee. I left that for later rather than guess at it again.

## What the hunt taught me

The ceiling went from ~31k to ~54k once the journal stopped gating admission, then to ~97k once delivery was sharded across cores, with correctness holding at every step.

Two lessons stuck with me, both about not trusting the obvious signal.

- **You can't predict the next bottleneck from the current one.** It was in turn a data firehose, async-runtime overhead, an I/O-coupling bug, a clone under a lock, a serial delivery stage, and finally frame count over frame size. Each fix changed what the next problem even was.
- **The profiler's hottest line is not the constraint.** Three times I fixed exactly what the flamegraph put on top and the knee didn't move at all — a thread pegged at 100% by spinning tells you nothing. The two fixes that did move it were the ones a sampling profiler couldn't see: an `fsync` asleep off-CPU, and a serial stage only `ss` could find.

The engine from part one still hasn't broken a sweat. Everything hard about making this exchange fast was the fan-out from one writer to many readers.
