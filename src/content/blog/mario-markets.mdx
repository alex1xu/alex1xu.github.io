Last winter, about 150 college students spent an afternoon trading binary contracts on prerecorded Mario Kart races while the standings updated on a projector at the front of the room. Real money, a live commentator calling the races, four markets open at once. I built the Python exchange behind it with a small student team on a five-week deadline, and I owned the matching engine and the performance work. It ran the event. Then, under load, it fell over — and I spent the months after rebuilding the whole thing from scratch in Rust, alone, to find out how much of that was Python's fault and how much was mine.

<Callout type="lesson" title="Short version, for the skim">
The Python exchange saturated somewhere south of **300 accepted orders a second**, with a p99 latency that climbed into the tens of *seconds* under load. The Rust rewrite sustains **20,000 orders a second** at an engine-side p99 of **33 microseconds** and has never dropped a message.

That's a 70×-ish gap, and it's the least interesting number here. Two things I didn't expect going in:

- **Almost none of the speedup came from "Rust is faster than Python."** When I broke each of v2's design choices on purpose and re-measured, the dominant lever was **never allocating on the hot path** (×5.3 throughput, ×64 tail latency). The clever array-shaped order book everyone diagrams? Worth ×1.12.
- **The rewrite had to get *more* correct, not less, to go fast.** The old system's matching core was fine the whole time. Everything that broke — lost orders, a double-settlement risk, races — lived in the distributed plumbing wrapped around it. Speed and correctness turned out to be the same discipline, not a tradeoff.

And a load-testing lesson that cost me a week: **a load test can lie to you about which side is slow.** Mine did, twice.
</Callout>

## The game, and why it needed a real exchange

The instrument is a Kalshi-style binary prediction market: one market per racer, asking "does this racer win?" You trade YES or NO contracts priced in integer cents on a 1–99 ladder, a YES and a NO always sum to 100¢, and at the end of the race the market settles to 0 or 100 depending on who won. Buy YES at 40¢, your racer wins, you're paid 100. Simple to state.

Underneath, it's a real exchange. Every market is a continuous limit order book with price-time priority: limit and market orders, partial fills, cancels, resting liquidity, a matching engine deciding who trades with whom and at what price. On top sit wallets, per-market P&L, and a cumulative leaderboard. Four races run at once, so four books are live simultaneously and 150 people are hitting all of them.

That last detail is the one that turns throughput and latency from abstractions into something you can watch fail. Picture 150 laptops firing orders at the same four books while the standings tick over on the projector and someone's shouting about a blue shell. A few hundred milliseconds of lag is the difference between a fill and a miss, and everyone can see the board. The exchange doesn't get to be slow, and it *really* doesn't get to be wrong about who has what — not with money on the line and a room watching.

It worked well enough to run the room. Then I spent the next few months finding out how much "well enough on the day" was hiding.

## Version one: Python, Redis, and a board game underneath

Here's an honest place to start: the repo didn't begin life as an exchange. I started it as a Snakes-and-Ladders board-game backend, and grafted the exchange on top — which is why to this day the database tables carry a `calshi_` prefix, a fossil from the thing it used to be. I mention it because it sets the tone. This was a system I assembled at speed out of what was already there.

The topology was a React frontend, a FastAPI backend, a standalone Python asyncio matching engine, Redis doing double duty as both the command bus and the realtime fanout, and Postgres as the authoritative state.

<OldTopology />

I built it CQRS from the first commit, and aggressively async. `POST /orders` returned `202 Accepted` immediately — it did *not* persist or match the order. It dropped the command onto a Redis stream and returned. The engine consumed the stream, matched in memory, then persisted to Postgres and published fills over pub/sub; the browser learned what happened when a WebSocket message told it to invalidate its cache and refetch.

That's a clean pattern on paper, and it has a consequence you feel immediately: the UI's view of your P&L always lags a refetch. "I placed an order but I don't see it yet" isn't a bug in that architecture, it's the architecture — I'd put the acknowledgment and the truth in different places, and they arrive at different times.

The matching *core* was fine, and I want to say that clearly because it's the whole surprise of the post-mortem later. One shared order book per market, a trade crosses when a YES bid and a NO bid sum to at least 100, FIFO price-time priority, fills at the resting order's price. I wrote that logic early and it stayed sound the entire time. The engine at the center was never really the problem. The blood was all in the plumbing I wrapped around it — the bus, the cache, the fanout, the places where two things had to agree and didn't.

### Where v1 grew: six eras in five weeks

I didn't design v1 and then build it. It grew, and the shape of the growth is the story. Six rough eras, from the commit log:

1. **A board game (late January).** Snakes and Ladders on a generic FastAPI "game" backend, with a market generator spinning binary markets off game state. The exchange core got grafted onto this chassis.
2. **The race pivot, and first deploy.** I dropped the live game for prerecorded Mario Kart races and stood the whole thing up on EC2 behind nginx. The order book was still a `SortedDict`.
3. **Optimize and add bots (mid-February).** Batch matching, a taking bot and a market-maker bot running inside the engine, `orjson`, smaller snapshots. This is where I started load-testing, and where the load-testing lie began.
4. **The balance rewrite.** I tore out a fragile `available + reserved` wallet model and replaced it with a single realized-P&L number. There's a commit literally titled `"SLIGHT BROKEN; rework of balance logic"`, which is exactly as reassuring as it sounds.
5. **The two-plane split.** The single biggest performance lever in v1's whole history: I made the engine run *purely in memory during a round* and bulk-persist only at the end, which killed the per-fill database write on the hot path. In the same era I split the one FastAPI app into a control plane and a separate WebSocket gateway, because one process couldn't own both order entry and realtime fanout under load.
6. **Squeeze toward the target (final days).** The `SortedDict` → bucket-array order book (below), three Redis reads collapsed into one, `@dataclass(slots=True)`, three WebSocket gateways behind nginx `least_conn`, an auth cache, and the WebSocket race that became my sharpest scar (BUG-005, also below).

Every era added a layer around the matching core to make it faster or more scalable. And every layer added a seam — a place where two components now had to agree about state. Keep count, because that's where all the bugs turned out to live.

### The first real win: a tree, then an array

One v1 optimization is worth slowing down for, because the entire rewrite is built on the same insight.

I started the order book as a `SortedDict` — a balanced tree keyed by price, O(log n) inserts and lookups at each level. That's the reflexive data structure for a book, and for a general exchange with unbounded prices it's the right one. But this exchange doesn't have unbounded prices. Contracts trade on a 1–99 cent ladder. The set of possible price levels is exactly 99 integers, known ahead of time, forever.

So the tree is pure overhead. If a price level is just an integer in `[1, 99]`, then a level *is* an array index. I replaced the `SortedDict` with a fixed 99-slot bucket array: inserting or looking up a level became O(1) direct indexing, and finding the best price became a small bounded backward scan across at most 99 slots. No comparisons, no tree balancing, no pointer chasing.

<ArrayLadder />

I want to be careful about what I can claim here, because it's exactly the kind of thing that's easy to wave at. I shipped this change, but I never benchmarked it in isolation on the old system — it landed in a batch of a dozen other optimizations in the final days. So the v1 speedup is **projected, not measured**: I know structurally that O(log n) level ops became O(1) and that a flat array of integers against a tree of boxed nodes isn't a close contest, but I don't have a clean before/after number from v1 to point at.

What I *do* have is the same idea, measured cleanly, because v2's order book is built on it — a flat array indexed by cent, this time with an intrusive linked list threading time priority through each level and an object-pool arena behind it so nothing allocates per order. When I later set out to find what actually made v2 fast, this array shape was the first thing I put on the scale. It surprised me: against a `BTreeMap` ladder with allocation held constant, the flat array bought a real but modest **~12% throughput** and *identical* tail latency. The famous trick was the smaller lever. I'll come back to what the big one was, because it inverts the story you'd expect — but the seed of the whole rewrite is this one v1 observation: you don't need a comparison-based data structure when your keys are 99 known integers.

## The wall: two ways a load test lied to me

I decided on the rewrite because the old system hit a wall under load. But the part I found most interesting, looking back, is that the load test that found the wall was lying to me — and telling the lie apart from the truth is the actual lesson.

I aimed for 1,500 aggregate orders a second: 150 users firing about 10 a second each. What happened under load was a *two-front* failure, and for a while I couldn't tell the two fronts apart.

**Front one: my load generator was the bottleneck, not the server.** I'd written the load client as a single Python process on one asyncio event loop — no `uvloop`, no multiprocessing. One process was opening 200-plus WebSocket connections, firing HTTP order submissions, *and* draining the inbound flood of book updates and trade notifications coming back, all on one core. Worse, I'd coupled the two: each simulated user awaited its own HTTP response before sending the next order. So when the server slowed down, my *client's* send rate collapsed right along with it — a closed loop that throttled itself to somewhere around 10–20% of target. It wasn't measuring the exchange's ceiling. It was measuring its own. I added a `--no-ws` flag to strip the inbound WebSocket load and measure the order path alone, and that's the point I realized the receiving side of my own test had been eating my throughput.

**Front two: underneath the throttled client, the server was also saturating.** Even at the sub-300 orders a second my crippled client could actually deliver, the engine couldn't keep up. One asyncio actor per market, four in all, topped out around **150–286 accepted orders a second**, with a submit-to-fill p99 between **28 and 78 seconds** and a backlog that climbed as high as **17,002** queued commands. I'd built a Grafana row called "Engine Queueing" to watch exactly this pileup, so it wasn't a surprise so much as a fear I'd instrumented and then confirmed.

<TwoFrontWall />

The honest reading is that I *couldn't* cleanly measure the server's ceiling with this setup, because the client capped at roughly where the server also gave out. The rate my throttled client could offer (~150–300/s) and the rate the server accepted before saturating (~286/s) landed at nearly the same magnitude — not two clean walls at different heights, but two failures entangled close enough that I couldn't separate them. Both were real, and both pointed the same way: this design couldn't take the load I built it for. The network was never close to the limit — WebSocket traffic peaked around 3.7 MB/s — so this was pure compute and coordination overhead, not bandwidth.

<Callout type="lesson" title="A closed-loop load test on one event loop measures the load test">
When one process drives connections, sends orders, and drains responses on a single event loop, a saturated generator and a saturated server produce the *same* symptom: the achieved rate flatlines below target. You have to rule out the client before you can trust the server number. And when both ceilings sit at nearly the same rate, as mine did, you can't cleanly separate them at all — you just know you're slow somewhere and you've blinded yourself to where. I learned to run the generator on separate hardware and, later, on separate *machines*. That comes back at the end.
</Callout>

## The case for a rewrite: it was never just speed

Speed was the visible problem. The scarier one was quieter, and I only really saw it while writing the post-mortem: I had almost no way to prove the old system hadn't corrupted itself.

The durability story was the sharpest edge. The engine's `persist_*` functions — the ones that write state to Postgres — had *zero live callers* during a round. The in-memory heap was the only truth, and the database got written only at settlement. A crash mid-round didn't lose the last few seconds; it lost the entire round. And settlement had two divergent code paths, one reading in-memory state and one reading Postgres directly, with no shared lock — so if the two ever disagreed about what someone held, I could double-pay a position. They had to agree by luck, not by construction.

The rest of the ledger was of a piece:

- The Redis control bus was untyped and unacknowledged — commands went out as loosely-shaped messages with no delivery guarantee and no schema enforcing what a valid command even looked like. A dropped or reordered `settle_round` would just silently fail to settle.
- The read path forked on a flag that chose Redis-cache-as-truth or Postgres-as-truth per endpoint. On a cold cache, one path returned zeros instead of falling back to the database — so "what's my balance" could quietly answer wrong.
- Concurrency was safe only by accident. The clearest case is **BUG-005**: once I sharded the WebSocket subscriber into multiple worker coroutines to scale fanout, they all read and wrote a shared per-channel sequence map with no lock, and raced. Sequence numbers clobbered each other and updates dropped or reordered. The fix — an `asyncio.Lock` — lives in one commit on a branch I named `ws-but-fixed-4real`. The branch name records how many tries it took.
- The docs had drifted from the code in ways that each hid a hazard: the position limit was hardcoded to 100, not the 200 the docs claimed; starting balances were 0, not the documented 100k; there was no ownership check on cancels, so nothing structurally stopped one user cancelling another's order.

Notice where every one of these lived: in the plumbing, never in the matching core. That's the meta-pattern of the whole post-mortem, and it lines up exactly with the six eras — each optimization I added to go faster introduced a seam, and each seam was where correctness eventually leaked.

<Callout type="lesson" title="The bugs cluster at the seams optimizations add">
The core algorithm was correct the whole time. What broke was everything I added *around* it to make it fast: the command bus, the cache-as-truth read path, the sharded fanout. Each optimization introduced a boundary where two components had to agree about state, and each boundary was a place they eventually didn't. Speeding a system up by distributing it means every new seam is a new correctness liability — and I'd added six eras of seams.
</Callout>

So the brief I wrote for the rewrite wasn't "make it fast." It was "make it fast *and* provably correct, as one discipline instead of a tradeoff." One source of truth instead of a cache and a database that fork. One transport instead of a bus bolted onto a pub/sub. Real backpressure instead of an unbounded backlog. Invariants enforced by the type system instead of by a comment and a hope. One thing to head off an easy misread: money was integer cents in *both* versions. This was never a floats-are-wrong story. The argument for the rewrite is durability, typed transport, and the absence of double-settlement — not arithmetic.

## Version two: one thread, no locks, one source of truth

The cleanest way to present the v2 design is as a list of answers, because each major decision is a direct response to a specific v1 failure.

<V1V2SideBySide />

**v1 raced state across coroutines and processes; v2 has a single writer.** One engine OS thread owns every order book and every wallet for the live round. No locks on the hot path, no shared mutable hot-path state — one thread does all the mutation, so nothing the engine touches while matching needs a lock. The immediate payoff is determinism: feed the engine the same sequence of commands and you get bit-for-bit identical output, every time. That property is what the entire correctness story later rests on.

**v1's notion of "who was first" was ambiguous across a sharded bus; v2 decides fairness at exactly one point.** A single ingress task assigns the global sequence number to every command as it arrives, at one location in the code. That sequence number *is* the fairness anchor — the order the engine sees commands is fixed there and never reconsidered. Price-time priority means nothing if two components disagree about the time, and v1 had several clocks; v2 has exactly one that matters.

**v1 used Redis as both bus and fanout, which is where BUG-005 lived; v2 uses lock-free rings.** Commands flow from ingress to the engine over a single-producer single-consumer ring, and events flow back out over another. The engine busy-spins on a dedicated thread reading its inbound ring and never touches an async runtime — no `await`, no scheduler, no futures, just a thread in a loop. I'll come back to how I convinced myself the ring is actually correct, because a hand-rolled lock-free queue is exactly the kind of thing that's subtly wrong for months.

**And no order allocates on the hot path.** Every resting order lives in a pre-sized object-pool arena; matching relinks slots through the intrusive list instead of asking the allocator for memory. I'm flagging this as a first-class design decision, not an implementation detail, because when I later benchmarked what makes v2 fast, this turned out to be the dominant lever — bigger than the array shape, bigger than anything else I isolated. The number comes later; the commitment is here.

<HotPath />

**v1 had no mid-round durability; v2 journals before the engine ever sees a command.** Ingress sequences each command, then hands it to the journal *before* pushing it onto the engine's inbound ring. The ordering is deliberate: nothing reaches the engine that isn't already queued for durability, so the write-ahead log can never be missing a command the engine acted on. `fsync` is batched via group-commit — one flush per batch, not one per command, on the journal's own thread about every 5 ms, off the engine's hot path. That batching isn't a small optimization: on a real disk it's the difference between roughly 500 and 32,000 commands a second, which I measure later.

I want to be precise about what that buys, because it's easy to over-read. A command is durable *in intent* — in the journal channel — before the engine matches it, but the actual disk `fsync` lands with the next group-commit up to a few milliseconds later. So the v2 acknowledgment is bounded-and-ordered durability, not "on disk the instant you're acked." It's the same *class* of tradeoff v1 had, except here the lag is bounded and the engine's input is journaled-in-intent first — which is the exact property that lets replay reconstruct a round.

<NewTopology />

**v1 had no collateral, just a ±100 position limit that bots skipped; v2 has one collateralized risk authority.** This is the one place the rewrite made a real *product* decision, so I'll flag it as a decision rather than an obvious win. v2 models risk the way a real exchange does: to hold a position you post collateral equal to your worst-case loss, and one shared authority reserves it at order entry — before the order can match, so a resting limit can never cross into a position its owner can't cover — settles against it on each fill, and releases it after. `reserve → match → settle → release`, with a per-market cap and a global budget. Real Kalshi is collateralized, so this is more faithful to the thing I was imitating. Whether it's the right call for a *party game* is a separate question I come back to at the end, because it changes how the game feels to play.

**And the whole thing is built to be extended without touching the hot path.** The instrument-specific logic lives behind four narrow traits — `Book`, `RiskModel`, `Settlement`, `Scoring` — so I could swap binary contracts for another instrument without going near the sequencer, the rings, or the journal. The test I hold an abstraction to is deletion: if I want to run a different kind of market next year, I write four trait impls and reuse everything else. That's a real seam, not a speculative one. All of it adds up to 16 `ob-*` library crates plus the `ob-server` binary — enough that "it's a real system" isn't a claim I have to argue.

## Proving it didn't break: a second engine, on purpose

Here's the problem with a fast, clever order book: the cleverness is exactly what makes it hard to trust. An intrusive linked list threaded through an object-pool arena, with cached best-price cursors and O(1) relinking, has a lot of moving parts a `SortedDict` doesn't — and every one is a place a subtle bug can hide and produce *almost* the right answer. I'd just spent a post-mortem learning that my last "it works on the day" system was quietly corruptible. I wasn't going to trust this one by reading it.

So I did the thing you do to trust a fast implementation: I raced it against a slow one that's obviously right, on every input, forever.

I wrote a second order book, `RefBook`, whose only design goal is to be correct in a way you can verify by reading it. Resting orders live in a flat `Vec`. To find the best order to match against, it rescans the *entire book* every single time and picks the best-priced, earliest one — O(n) per operation, embarrassingly slow, and impossible to get subtly wrong. It lives in its own crate and shares *no code* with the production ladder beyond the domain contract they both implement. That's the point. If the two shared a matching helper, a bug in that helper would corrupt both books identically and the comparison would pass while both were wrong. Sharing nothing means a bug in the fast book shows up as a *divergence* from the slow one.

The differential harness feeds an identical, randomly generated command stream to both books and, after *every single command*, asserts the two produced the exact same event stream and that their resting-order snapshots match.

<CodeCard file="differential.rs" lines="151–222">
```rust
let prod_ev = prod.apply(cmd.clone(), seq, ts, &mut prod_risk);
let ref_ev  = refb.apply(cmd,         seq, ts, &mut ref_risk);
prop_assert_eq!(prod_ev.as_slice(), ref_ev.as_slice(), "events diverged at step {}", i);
prop_assert_eq!(prod.resting_snapshot(), refb.resting_snapshot(), "book diverged at step {}", i);
```
</CodeCard>

<DifferentialHarness />

It runs 600 generated cases, and I made the command generator deliberately cruel: a tiny space of four users and eight client-order-ids, so crossing trades and self-trade-prevention fire constantly instead of almost never, roughly six orders per cancel, every mix of limit/market and GTC/IOC/FOK. On top of the event-equality check, each step asserts four cross-cutting invariants directly on the production book: contract and position conservation (net positions sum to zero per market — every contract someone is long, someone else is short), reserved collateral never goes negative, the book is never crossed at rest, and the production book's reserved collateral agrees with the reference book's. A bug that keeps the event streams equal but corrupts an invariant still gets caught.

Determinism gets the same treatment. Because the engine is a pure function of its sequenced input, and the journal is a write-ahead log of exactly that input and nothing else, replaying the journal has to reproduce the run bit-for-bit. The replay proptest steps the original and replayed streams in lockstep and asserts equality *after every command*, not just at the end — so a divergence gets caught mid-round while the books and wallets are still full, not after settlement has wiped them. One subtlety I had to get right: the arena's physical layout isn't canonical — two logically identical books can differ in which arena slots hold which orders — so I verify replay against a *normalized* snapshot that captures only logical state (resting orders in price-time order, positions, wallets, scoreboard), built from ordered maps so the comparison itself is deterministic.

And the ring — the one piece of genuinely subtle concurrent code — is model-checked. Under `loom`, the acquire/release protocol between producer and consumer is checked across *all* possible thread interleavings on a tiny two-slot ring, asserting the consumer sees every item with no loss and no reordering. `loom`'s state space explodes combinatorially, which is why the model stays microscopic; a separate one-million-item real-thread stress test is the probabilistic complement that catches what a tiny model can't reach.

<Callout type="lesson" title="To trust a clever data structure, diff it against a dumb one on every input">
The production book is fast because it's clever; the reference book is trustworthy because it's stupid. Running both on the same random stream and asserting equality after every command turns "is my intrusive-linked-list arena correct?" — a question I can't answer by staring at it — into "do these two disagree?", which a proptest answers in seconds. The reference book being slow is the feature: it shares no cleverness, so it shares no bugs.
</Callout>

The receipt is **517 of 517 tests passing** under `cargo test --workspace --all-features` — the 600-case differential, the replay determinism check, the ring model, and the rest, all green. Set that against v1, where the cross-process money path was simply untested. The fast engine is checked event-for-event against an independent oracle on every input; the old one took it on faith.

## How fast, and what actually binds it

The numbers, stated flat: **20,000 orders a second sustained**, in-system p50 of **2 microseconds** and p99 of **33 microseconds**, an engine loop averaging about 1.4 microseconds per iteration, and zero messages lost. The rig, because the numbers are meaningless without it: AWS `c7a.2xlarge` boxes (8 vCPU Intel Xeon 8488C, 16 GiB), a single market, a roster of 250 WebSocket clients. In-system latency is measured on the engine's own clock as `emit_ts − ingress_ts`, so it's immune to clock skew between machines.

The finding I actually care about isn't the throughput number. It's *where the bottleneck was* — and finding that meant taking the engine off the wire entirely.

So I ran the matching loop in-process, feeding it commands directly with no network and no egress, thread-pinned to one core. In isolation the engine processes a command in **p50 2.05 µs** (mean 1.27–1.42 µs across seeds) and sustains **630,000–690,000 commands a second**. That's the same ~2 µs scale as the wire run's engine clock — which means the 20k/s I measured on the wire was not the engine giving out. The engine was idling at a fraction of its capacity while something downstream throttled it. That used to be an inference from ring-depth telemetry; now it's a direct measurement.

The wire telemetry agrees. At every rate I drove the rig — nominally up to 150,000 orders a second — the inbound ring depth stayed at zero. The engine kept up with everything it was offered, which was only ever about 27,000 commands a second, because something upstream of the engine's *output* throttled the whole pipeline. Between the in-process 630k+ and the wire ring-depth pinned at zero, both measurements say the same thing: nothing on the ingress-to-match path was the wall.

The wall is on the output side, and I want to be exact about where, because I got this wrong in an earlier draft of my own mental model. The engine publishes each event into an outbound ring; a projection task drains that ring and fans every event out to the 250 per-client delivery lanes. Each reliable lane is capped, and when a client can't keep up, its lane fills — and rather than silently drop a message, the projection *evicts* that client. Past the knee, every one of the 250 clients overflows its lane and gets evicted at once. Here's the part that corrected me: I'd assumed the engine would end up spinning on a full *outbound ring*, waiting for the projection to drain it. It never does. Across the whole ladder, the engine's `outbound_full` counter stayed at **zero** while evictions hit **250**. The projection drains the ring faster than the engine fills it; the backup is entirely further downstream, at the per-client lanes. The engine that "never blocks" genuinely never blocked — not even on egress.

| offered/s | achieved/s | in-sys p99 | evictions |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2,000 | 1,795 | 16 µs | 0 |
| 10,000 | 8,973 | 16 µs | 0 |
| 20,000 | 17,974 | 33 µs | 0 |
| 25,000 | 20,451 | 4.2 ms | **250** |
| 150,000 | 20,941 | 4.2 ms | **250** |

The ladder reads cleanly once you know what you're looking at: microsecond latency and zero evictions through 20k/s, then a cliff at 25k where all 250 clients evict at once and the client-observed p99 jumps into the low milliseconds. That jump is *not* the engine slowing down at matching. With ring depth zero, a ~1.4 µs loop, and `outbound_full` still at zero, nothing on the ingress-to-match path produces milliseconds. It's the fan-out to 250 subscribers falling behind, the lanes overflowing, and the round-trip a client sees inflating while the engine underneath stays at ~16 µs. This is a *system* ceiling imposed by egress delivery, and it is not an engine matching ceiling.

### I built a second rig to find the real ceiling, and was half right

The obvious objection to all of that: maybe 20k was just my single load box again — the v1 mistake, round two. My design spec targets at least **50,000 orders a second on one engine core**, and the in-process bench says the engine *step* clears that six times over — but that's the engine in a vacuum, not the full wire path with real egress. I hadn't proven the wire ceiling, because on a single load box the egress-plus-load-box limit binds first and I couldn't see past it.

So I built the thing I'd wished for during the v1 post-mortem: a distributed load generator. A coordinator hands disjoint slices of the 250-client roster to worker processes on *separate machines*, each drives its slice, and the coordinator merges their latency histograms exactly (power-of-two buckets sum losslessly; quantiles get recomputed on the merged histogram, never averaged). Then I ran one engine box against three worker boxes, same 8-vCPU instance class, to take the load generator off the critical path once and for all.

I was half right, and the half I was wrong about is the interesting one. Three worker boxes did *not* move the plateau — it stayed right around 20,500 orders a second. So the wall was never my single load box. But it also wasn't the engine: across every rate up to 150,000 offered aggregate, the engine's `outbound_full` stayed at zero and its inbound ring depth stayed at zero, while `egress_evictions` pinned at 250. The bottleneck is the fan-out itself — delivering every fill and book update to 250 reliable subscribers fast enough — and that binds on the engine box's egress regardless of how many boxes push orders in. Adding senders doesn't help when the constraint is the number of *receivers* you have to keep in sync.

Which means the engine's true single-core wire ceiling is still a floor, not a number. I've measured that it sustains at least ~27,000 commands a second through the pipeline without ever backing up, and 630k+ in a vacuum, but the ≥50k/s *wire* figure remains unproven — the egress fan-out is a lower wall, and I'd rather say that plainly than quote the in-vacuum number as if it were the wire result. The honest state is: the engine has never been the thing that saturates, on either rig, and I now know the next wall by name.

One trap I'll flag so nobody else hits it: my harness has a built-in `sweep` mode that once reported 124,000 acked/s, and that number is *garbage* — a dedup-cache artifact where repeated client-order-ids after the first were answered from cache without ever reaching the engine. I don't cite it and neither should you. The restart-per-rate ladders above are the trustworthy view.

### So how much of "faster than Python" is real?

The defensible comparison is v1's *measured* saturation (150–286 accepted orders a second, first-hand from the old load-test artifacts) against v2's *measured* sustained rate (20,000, first-hand). That gap is real, and it's about 70×. What I won't claim is a clean apples-to-apples: the two ran on different hardware, and any projection of what an *optimized* v1 could have done is exactly that, a projection. The number I'll stand behind is "the old one fell over in the low hundreds and the new one doesn't at 20k."

## Which design choice actually mattered: break each one and re-measure

I still owed myself the *why*. Knowing the wire couldn't see the engine's ceiling, I stopped trying to measure it through the network and asked a different question in-process: of the choices that make v2's engine fast, *which one actually matters?*

The way to answer that is to break each one on purpose. I built regressed variants — undo exactly one design decision, hold everything else fixed, replay an identical five-million-command stream — and measured the delta. Every variant, run through the differential harness, produced a byte-identical **2,706,139 fills**, which confirms the input was identical across cells. (It's one seed, so there are no run-to-run variance bars — the ablation changed only speed, never the answer, which is exactly the guarantee the reference book buys.)

The profiler kept pointing at allocation, so I started there. The clean way to isolate the arena is to hold the book shape constant and flip only the allocator — the bottom two rows below, both `BTreeMap`, one on the pooled arena and one going back to allocating each price level on the heap.

| variant | book shape | hot-path alloc | throughput | p50 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| shipped | flat array | pooled arena | 2,207,254/s | 512 ns | 2,048 ns |
| swap array → `BTreeMap` | `BTreeMap` | pooled arena | 1,973,341/s | 512 ns | 2,048 ns |
| also arena → heap | `BTreeMap` | per-level heap | 375,194/s | 1,024 ns | 131,072 ns |

Dropping the arena — those bottom two rows, book shape fixed — cost **×5.3 in throughput** (1,973,341 → 375,194) and **×64 in tail latency**, p99 from 2 µs to 131 µs. That's the dominant lever by a wide margin, and it's the one the folklore doesn't lead with. The reason the tail specifically detonates: an allocator call can wander off into unbounded work — coalescing free lists, a syscall into the kernel for more memory — while handing out a pre-reserved pool slot is bounded and constant-time every time. That's why removing the arena wrecks p99, not just the mean.

Then I checked the famous trick, the array-vs-tree shape, with allocation held constant — the top two rows. The flat array bought **×1.12 throughput and identical p50/p99**. Real, worth keeping, and a small fraction of the allocation win: the arena mattered something like thirty times more. The shape is the part I was proud of on the old exchange, the part worth a diagram. The measurement says the arena underneath it was doing most of the work.

<AblationBars />

One caveat on reading the latency columns: these histograms use power-of-two buckets (512 / 1,024 / 2,048 / 131,072 ns are exactly 2⁹ / 2¹⁰ / 2¹¹ / 2¹⁷), so p50 and p99 land on bucket edges and the clean ×2 and ×64 latency ratios are coarse — bucket-resolution artifacts, not precise measurements. Throughput is the precise signal; latency is bucketed.

Then the journal, on a real xfs disk. (The first time I ran this it landed in `/tmp`, which was tmpfs — RAM — so `fsync` was a no-op and the result was meaningless. The honest number needs a real disk target, which cost me a re-run to notice.)

| journal commit | throughput | p50 |
|---|---:|---:|
| group-commit (batch of 64) | 32,299/s | 0.26 µs |
| per-command `fsync` | 508/s | 2.10 ms |

Group-commit is **×63.5**. Per-command durability means a real 2.10 ms disk `fsync` on the path of every single order; batching amortizes it to nothing. That's the concrete cost behind "durability off the hot path" — not a figure of speech, but 2 ms per order if you get it wrong.

One honesty note on what I *didn't* measure: I never isolated the lock-free ring against a mutex-guarded book as a throughput ablation. The single-writer ring design earns its place a different way — the inbound-ring depth staying at zero under load, plus the `loom` model check — not a regressed branch. So the measured trio is allocation, shape, and journal; the ring stands on correctness evidence, not a speed delta.

<Callout type="lesson" title="The biggest lever was the one I wasn't bragging about">
The array-shaped order book is the clever part, the part worth a diagram — and when I finally measured it, it was worth 12%. Not allocating on the hot path was worth 5.3× and a 64× better tail. The lesson isn't "shape doesn't matter"; it's that the win you can *see* in the data-structure diagram is rarely the win that dominates the profile. I only know which is which because I broke each one on purpose and re-measured, instead of trusting the story I'd have told from the design.
</Callout>

## Competition day, and what I'd measure next

The original ran the actual event. 150 traders, real money, four live races, standings on the projector — and it held together, on a deploy path propped up by a watchdog script and a cloudflared quick-tunnel I would not describe as robust. I say that plainly because the rewrite is not a dunk on the thing that shipped. Shipping a working exchange for a room of 150 people on a five-week deadline is the reason there was anything worth rewriting. The v2 story only exists because v1 ran the race first.

The design decision I sat with longest is the risk model, because it's the one place engineering-correct and game-correct pull apart. v2's collateralized worst-case-loss model is more faithful to how a real prediction market works — you post what you could lose, you can't take a position you can't cover. But the competition ran on v1's model: a flat ±100 position limit, no collateral, bots exempt. And that difference changes how the game *feels*. Under a position limit, a new player walks up and immediately takes a swing to the cap without thinking about margin; under collateral, they have to understand reserve-and-settle before they can do anything — more correct, and less fun at a party. I went with collateral anyway, and the reason is the whole thesis of the rewrite in miniature: I'd rather the engine be the faithful thing and let the *event* dial the fun back in on top of it. The four-trait seam is what makes that cheap — `RiskModel` is one impl, so a position-limit variant is a drop-in for whoever wants the party-game feel back, without touching the engine that earns the correctness guarantees.

Here's the honest ledger of what's proven versus not:

| Dimension | v1 (Python / Redis) | v2 (Rust) |
|---|---|---|
| Topology | 4 processes over a Redis bus, CQRS | 1 process, 1 machine, lock-free rings |
| Order book | `SortedDict` → 99-slot bucket array | flat array ladder + arena, O(1) hot path |
| Concurrency | 1 async actor/market (4 total), shared state | single writer, SPSC rings, busy-spin |
| Durability | none mid-round (heap is truth) | journaled WAL, replay verified every command |
| Correctness | cross-process money path untested | 517/517, 600-case differential + replay |
| Risk model | ±100 position limit, bots exempt | collateralized worst-case loss |
| Measured perf | saturates ~150–286/s, p99 28–78 s | 20k/s sustained, p99 33 µs, 0 loss; 630k+/s in-process |
| What the design buys | — | arena vs heap ×5.3 tput / ×64 p99; array vs tree ×1.12; group-commit ×63.5 |
| Engine ceiling | — | ~2 µs/command; wall is egress fan-out, not the engine |

Proven: 20k/s sustained with zero loss; 517/517 tests including the differential and replay determinism checks; that the engine never bottlenecked on input on *either* rig; and — measured by breaking each choice on purpose — that not allocating on the hot path is the dominant speed lever (×5.3), the array shape a real but secondary one (×1.12), and group-commit worth ×63.5 on real disk. Not proven: the ≥50k/s single-core *wire* ceiling. The distributed run told me *why* I can't see it yet — egress fan-out to 250 subscribers binds first — so the next experiment is to push the fan-out itself, not the engine. I'd rather list that open than let it read as finished.

If there's one thing this whole rewrite argues, it's that the speed didn't come from Rust being faster than Python. It came from the design — and when I measured the design, the biggest single piece of it was the least glamorous one: never allocating while matching. The rest is a shape where correctness and speed turned out to be the *same* discipline. A single writer that's both race-free and lock-free. One sequencing point that's both fair and cheap. A journal that's durable precisely because it never touches the hot path. An arena that's fast precisely because it never touches the allocator. And an oracle that proves correctness on every command the engine will ever be fast enough to process. Going fast *was* going correct.

The code is public — clone it, run the same harness, and you'll get the same plots. And when I finally push the fan-out hard enough to see where the engine actually tops out on the wire, that's the next post.
