Last winter, about 150 college students spent an afternoon trading binary contracts on prerecorded Mario Kart races while the standings updated on a projector at the front of the room. There was real money on the line and a live commentator calling the races. The Python exchange behind it worked — and then, under load, it didn't.

I spent the months after rebuilding it from scratch in Rust. The rewrite sustains 20,000 orders a second at a p99 of 66 microseconds and has never dropped a message; the original saturated somewhere south of 300 accepted orders a second with a p99 that climbed into the tens of *seconds*. That's the headline, and it's the least interesting part. A trading engine that's fast but occasionally loses an order, double-settles a position, or serves stale P&L is worse than a slow one — in a live competition with real money, someone notices. So this is really two stories braided together: going 70–130× faster, and having to become *more* correct, not less, to do it. Almost none of the speed came from the language — and when I finally put v2's design choices on a scale, the single biggest lever wasn't the clever array-shaped order book everyone reaches for. It was never allocating on the hot path.

## The game, and why it needed a real exchange

The instrument is a Kalshi-style binary prediction market: one market per racer, asking "does this racer win?" You trade YES or NO contracts priced in integer cents on a 1–99 ladder, where a YES and a NO always sum to 100¢, and at the end of the race the market settles to 0 or 100 depending on the outcome. Buy YES at 40¢, the racer wins, you're paid 100 — that's the whole payoff structure. Simple to state, and exactly the shape of a real exchange underneath.

Because it *is* a real exchange underneath. Every market is a continuous limit order book with price-time priority: limit and market orders, partial fills, cancels, resting liquidity, a matching engine deciding who trades with whom and at what price. On top of that sit wallets, per-market P&L, and a cumulative leaderboard, with four races open at once so four books are live simultaneously and 150 people are hitting all of them. That last detail is the one that matters — throughput and latency stop being abstractions the moment you picture 150 laptops firing orders at the same four books while the standings tick over on the projector and someone's shouting about a blue shell. A few hundred milliseconds of lag is the difference between a fill and a miss, and everyone can see the board.

The first version shipped and ran the event. It was built by a small student team on a five-week deadline, and I drove the matching engine, the performance work, and the exchange mechanics. It worked well enough to run a room of 150 traders — and then I spent the next few months rebuilding the whole thing alone in Rust, because "worked well enough on the day" turned out to hide a lot.

## Version one: Python, Redis, and a board game underneath

Here's an honest detail to start with: the repo didn't begin life as an exchange. It started as a Snakes-and-Ladders board-game backend, and the exchange got grafted on top, which is why to this day the database tables carry a `calshi_` prefix — a fossil from the thing it used to be. I mention it not to dunk on it but because it sets the tone. This was a system assembled at speed out of what was already there.

The topology was a React frontend, a FastAPI backend, a standalone Python asyncio matching engine, Redis doing double duty as both the command bus (Streams) and the realtime fanout (pub/sub), and Postgres as the authoritative state.

<OldTopology />

The design was CQRS from the very first commit, and aggressively async. `POST /orders` returned `202 Accepted` immediately — it did *not* persist the order or match it. It dropped the command onto a Redis stream and returned. The engine consumed the stream, matched in memory, then persisted the result to Postgres and published fills over pub/sub; the browser learned what happened when a WebSocket message told it to invalidate its query cache and refetch. This is a clean pattern on paper, and it has a built-in consequence you feel immediately: eventual consistency. The UI's view of your P&L always lags a refetch. "I placed an order but I don't see it yet" isn't a bug in that architecture, it's the architecture — the acknowledgment and the truth live in different places and arrive at different times.

The thing worth saying clearly is that the matching *core* was fine. One shared order book per market, a trade crosses when a YES bid and a NO bid sum to at least 100, FIFO price-time priority, and fills happen at the resting order's price. That logic was sound and stayed sound. Keep this in mind, because it's the whole surprise of the post-mortem later: the engine at the center was never really the problem. The blood was all in the plumbing wrapped around it — the bus, the cache, the fanout, the places where two things had to agree and didn't.

## The first real win: a tree, then an array

The one performance optimization from v1 that's genuinely worth teaching is also the insight the entire rewrite is built on, so it's worth slowing down for.

The order book started as a `SortedDict` — a balanced tree keyed by price, giving O(log n) inserts and lookups at each price level. That's the reflexive data structure for a book, and for a general exchange with unbounded prices it's the right one. But this exchange doesn't have unbounded prices. Contracts trade on a 1–99 cent ladder. The set of possible price levels is exactly 99 integers, known ahead of time, forever.

So the tree is pure overhead. If a price level is just an integer in `[1, 99]`, then a level *is* an array index. On the old exchange I replaced the `SortedDict` with a fixed 99-slot bucket array: inserting or looking up a level is O(1) direct indexing, and finding the best price is a small bounded backward scan from where you last saw it (`_rescan_best`) across at most 99 slots. No comparisons, no tree balancing, no pointer chasing. That was a real, shipped progression on the old repo, and the entire trick is one observation: you don't need a comparison-based data structure when your key space is 99 known integers.

<ArrayLadder />

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the kind of claim that's easy to wave at. The per-operation latency win from that change on v1 was the documented *goal* of the optimization, but it was never benchmarked in isolation on the old system — so for v1 I label the speedup **projected**, not measured. What I can say structurally is that O(log n) level ops became O(1), and the constant factors of a flat array against a tree of boxed nodes are not close.

What makes this satisfying is that I later got to measure the *same idea* cleanly, because v2's order book is built on precisely this insight — a flat array indexed by cent, which I called the `ArrayLadder`, this time with an intrusive linked list threading time priority through each level and an object-pool arena behind it so there's no per-order allocation at all. And when I set out to find what actually made v2 fast, this array shape was the first thing I put on the scale. The result surprised me: against a `BTreeMap` ladder with allocation held constant, the flat array bought a real but modest **~12% throughput** and *identical* tail latency. The famous trick was the smaller lever. The bigger one — by far — was the arena underneath it: never allocating on the hot path. I get to the full measured breakdown in §8, but the headline is worth planting here, because it inverts the story you'd expect: the array shape is the part everyone talks about, and it's not where the speed lives.

## The wall: two ways a load test can lie

The rewrite happened because the old system hit a wall under load. But the genuinely interesting thing — the part I'd point an interviewer at — is that the load test that found the wall was lying to me, and disentangling the lie from the truth is the actual lesson.

The targets were modest by exchange standards and appropriate for the event: the roadmap wanted 1,000 requests a second, and the saturation run aimed for 1,500 aggregate orders a second — 150 users firing about 10 a second each. What actually happened under load was a *two-front* failure, and for a while I couldn't tell the two fronts apart.

**Front one: the load generator was the bottleneck, not the server.** The load client was a single Python process on a single asyncio event loop — no `uvloop`, no multiprocessing. One process was opening 200-plus WebSocket connections, firing HTTP order submissions, *and* draining the inbound flood of book updates and trade notifications coming back, all on one GIL-bound core. That core saturated long before the server did, and the client quietly throttled itself to somewhere around 10–20% of the target — call it 150–300 orders a second actually offered. It wasn't measuring the exchange's ceiling; it was measuring its own. The telltale sign was a `--no-ws` flag that existed specifically to stop draining inbound traffic and measure HTTP submission alone — which is a flag you only add once you've realized the receiving side of your own load test is eating your throughput.

**Front two: underneath the throttled client, the server was also saturating.** The single-threaded engine — one asyncio actor per market, four in all — topped out at roughly **150–286 accepted orders a second**, with a p99 latency between **28 and 78 seconds** and an order backlog that climbed as high as **17,002** queued commands. There was a Grafana row labeled "Engine Queueing" built to watch exactly this pileup, which tells you it was a known failure mode, not a surprise.

<TwoFrontWall />

The honest reading is that you *cannot* cleanly measure the server's ceiling with this setup, because the client caps at roughly where the server also gives out. The offered rate the throttled client could sustain (~150–300/s) and the rate the server actually accepted before saturating (~286/s) land at nearly the same magnitude — these aren't two clean walls at different heights, they're two failures entangled close enough that I couldn't separate them. What I *can* say is that both were real and both pointed the same way: this design can't take the load it was built for. The network was never close to the limit — measured WebSocket traffic peaked around 2–3.7 MB/s — so this was pure compute and coordination overhead, not bandwidth.

<Callout type="lesson" title="A closed-loop load test on one event loop measures the load test">
When your load generator drives connections, sends orders, and drains responses all on a single event loop, a saturated generator and a saturated server produce the *same* symptom: the achieved rate flatlines below target. You have to rule out the client before you can trust the server number — and if you can't run the generator on separate hardware, you at least have to know which ceiling you're looking at. When both ceilings sit at nearly the same rate, as mine did, you can't cleanly separate them at all.
</Callout>

## The case for a rewrite: it was never just speed

Speed was the visible problem. The scarier one was quieter, and I only really saw it while writing the post-mortem: the old system had almost no way to prove it hadn't corrupted itself.

The durability story was the sharpest edge. The engine's `persist_*` functions — the ones that write state to Postgres — had *zero live callers* during a round. The in-memory heap was the only truth, and the database got written only at settlement. A crash mid-round didn't lose the last few seconds; it lost the entire round. And because settlement had two divergent code paths — one reading in-memory state, one reading Postgres — there was a live risk of double-paying a position if the two ever disagreed about what someone held.

The rest of the ledger was of a piece:

- The Redis control bus was untyped and unacknowledged — commands went out as loosely-shaped messages with no delivery guarantee and no schema enforcing what a valid command even looked like.
- The read path forked on a `live_read_source` flag that chose Redis-cache-as-truth or Postgres-as-truth per endpoint, so "what's my balance" could be answered by two different sources that could disagree.
- Concurrency was an afterthought. The clearest example is BUG-005: a per-channel WebSocket sequence map was mutated from several worker coroutines with no lock, so sequence numbers could race and corrupt. The root-cause fix — an `asyncio.Lock` — lives in a single commit, `e1778f8`, on a branch someone named `ws-but-fixed-4real`. The branch name is the whole story.
- Documentation had drifted from code in ways that each hid a real hazard: the position limit was hardcoded to 100, not the 200 the docs claimed; starting balances were 0, not the documented 100k; there was no ownership check on cancels, so nothing structurally stopped one user cancelling another's order; the "49 tests" in the docs were actually 60. Small, except the cross-process money path underneath them was untested.

Every one of these lived in the plumbing, never in the matching core — which is the meta-pattern of the whole post-mortem.

<Callout type="lesson" title="The bugs cluster at the seams optimizations add">
The core algorithm was correct the whole time. What broke was everything added *around* it to make it fast: the command bus, the cache-as-truth read path, the multi-coroutine fanout. Each optimization introduced a seam where two components had to agree about state, and each seam was a place they eventually didn't. Speeding a system up by distributing it means every new boundary is a new correctness liability.
</Callout>

So the design brief for the rewrite wasn't "make it fast." It was "make it fast *and* provably correct, as one discipline instead of a tradeoff." One source of truth instead of a cache and a database that fork. One transport instead of a bus bolted onto a pub/sub. Real backpressure instead of an unbounded backlog. Invariants enforced by the type system instead of by a `# type: ignore` and a hope. One note before the design, to kill an easy misread: money was integer cents in *both* versions. This was never a floats-are-wrong story. The argument for the rewrite is durability, typed transport, and the absence of double-settlement — not arithmetic.

## Version two: one thread, no locks, one source of truth

The cleanest way to present the v2 design is as a list of answers — each major decision is the direct response to a specific v1 failure.

**v1 raced state across coroutines and processes; v2 has a single writer.** One engine OS thread owns every order book and every wallet for the live round. There are no locks on the hot path and no shared mutable *hot-path* state — one thread does all the mutation, so nothing the engine touches while matching needs a lock (the rings themselves are `Arc`-shared, but only the cells, never the book state). The immediate payoff is determinism: feed the engine the same sequence of commands and you get bit-for-bit identical output, every time, which is the property the entire correctness story later rests on.

**v1's notion of "who was first" was ambiguous across a sharded bus; v2 decides fairness at exactly one point.** A single ingress task assigns the global sequence number to every command as it arrives, at one location in the code. That sequence number *is* the fairness anchor — the order in which the engine sees commands is fixed there and never reconsidered. Price-time priority means nothing if two components disagree about the time; v2 has exactly one clock that matters.

**v1 used Redis as both bus and fanout, which is where BUG-005 lived; v2 uses lock-free rings.** Commands flow from ingress to the engine over a single-producer single-consumer ring, and events flow back out over another. The engine busy-spins on a dedicated thread reading its inbound ring and never touches an async runtime — no `await`, no scheduler, no futures, just a thread in a loop. I'll come back to how I convinced myself the ring is actually correct, because a hand-rolled lock-free queue is exactly the kind of thing that's subtly wrong for months.

**And no order allocates on the hot path.** Every resting order lives in a pre-sized object-pool arena; matching relinks slots through the intrusive list instead of asking the allocator for memory. I'm flagging this as a first-class design decision rather than an implementation detail, because when I benchmarked what makes v2 fast, this turned out to be the dominant lever — bigger than the array shape, bigger than anything else I isolated. The number is in §8; the design commitment is here.

**v1 had no mid-round durability; v2 journals before the engine ever sees a command.** Ingress sequences each command, then hands it to the journal channel *before* pushing it onto the engine's inbound ring. This ordering is deliberate: nothing reaches the engine that isn't already queued for durability, so the write-ahead log can never be missing a command the engine acted on. The journal channel is bounded, so in principle a stalled journal could transitively backpressure ingress and starve the engine — but the two are decoupled by a deep buffer and a group-commit flush, not by being "off the path." `fsync` is batched via group-commit so durability costs one flush per batch, not one per command; the flush happens on the journal's own thread about every 5 ms, off the engine's hot path. That batching is not a small optimization — on a real disk it's the difference between roughly 500 and 32,000 commands a second, which I quantify in §8.

That buys ordering, not the strong per-command durability guarantee you might read into it. A command enters the journal channel — durable *in intent* — before the engine matches it, but the actual disk `fsync` lands with the next group-commit up to a few milliseconds later. So v2's ack is bounded-and-ordered durability, not "durable on disk the instant you're acked." It's the same class of tradeoff v1 had, except here the lag is bounded and the engine input is journaled-in-intent first — which is the property that lets replay reconstruct the round.

<NewTopology />

**v1 had no collateral — just a ±100 position limit that bots skipped entirely; v2 has one collateralized risk authority.** This is the one place the rewrite made a real *product* decision, not just an engineering one, so I want to flag it as a decision rather than an obvious win. v1's only pre-trade risk gate was a hardcoded ±100 contract position limit, and bots were exempt from it. v2 models risk the way a real exchange does: to hold a position you post collateral equal to your worst-case loss, and the engine reserves it at order entry — before the order can match, so a resting limit can never cross into a position its owner can't cover — settles against it on each fill, and releases it after: `reserve → match → settle → release`, with a per-market cap and a global budget, all enforced by one authority shared across every book. Real Kalshi is collateralized, so this is genuinely more faithful to the thing we were imitating. Whether it's the right call for a *party game* is a separate question I'll come back to at the end, because it changes how the game feels to play.

**And the whole thing is built to be extended without touching the hot path.** The instrument-specific logic lives behind four narrow traits — `Book`, `RiskModel`, `Settlement`, `Scoring` — so you could swap binary contracts for some other instrument without going near the sequencer, the rings, or the journal. The deletion test for whether that abstraction earns its keep: if I want to run a totally different kind of market next year, I write four trait impls and reuse everything else. That's a real seam, not a speculative one. All of it adds up to 15 `ob-*` library crates plus the `ob-server` binary — enough that "it's a real system" isn't a claim I have to argue.

## Proving it didn't break: a second engine, on purpose

Here's the problem with a fast, clever order book: the cleverness is exactly what makes it hard to trust. An intrusive linked list threaded through an object-pool arena, with cached best-price cursors and O(1) relinking, has a lot of moving parts that a `SortedDict` doesn't — and every one is a place a subtle bug can hide and produce *almost* the right answer.

So the way you trust a fast thing is to race it against a slow thing that's obviously right, on every input, forever.

I wrote a second order book, `RefBook`, whose only design goal is being correct in a way you can verify by reading it. Resting orders live in a flat `Vec`. To find the best order to match against, it rescans the *entire book* every single time and picks the best-priced, earliest one — O(n) per operation, embarrassingly slow, and impossible to get subtly wrong. Crucially, `RefBook` shares *no code* with the production ladder beyond the domain contract they both implement. That's the point: if the two shared a matching helper, a bug in that helper would corrupt both books identically and the comparison would pass while both were wrong. By sharing nothing, a bug in the fast book shows up as a *divergence* from the slow one.

The differential harness feeds an identical, randomly generated command stream to both books and, after *every single command*, asserts that the two produced the exact same event stream and that their resting-order snapshots match.

<CodeCard file="differential.rs" lines="151–222">
```rust
let prod_ev = prod.apply(cmd.clone(), seq, ts, &mut prod_risk);
let ref_ev  = refb.apply(cmd,         seq, ts, &mut ref_risk);
prop_assert_eq!(prod_ev.as_slice(), ref_ev.as_slice(), "events diverged at step {}", i);
prop_assert_eq!(prod.resting_snapshot(), refb.resting_snapshot(), "book diverged at step {}", i);
```
</CodeCard>

<DifferentialHarness />

It runs 600 generated cases, and the command generator is deliberately cruel: a tiny space of four users and eight client-order-ids, so that crossing trades and self-trade-prevention fire constantly instead of almost never, roughly six orders per cancel, every mix of limit/market and GTC/IOC/FOK. (Self-trade prevention cancels the taker's own resting maker order when an incoming order would match against it — you can't trade with yourself.) On top of the event-equality check, each step asserts four cross-cutting invariants directly on the production book: contract and position **conservation** (net positions sum to zero per market — every contract someone is long, someone else is short), reserved collateral is **never negative**, the book is **never crossed** at rest (best bid strictly below best ask), and the production book's reserved collateral **agrees with the reference book's**. Note what's *not* here: the harness runs both books against a no-caps, never-rejects risk double, so there's no position-cap invariant to check — a cap breach isn't even expressible in this setup. A bug that keeps the event streams equal but corrupts an invariant still gets caught.

Determinism gets the same treatment. Because the engine is a pure function of its sequenced input, and the journal is a write-ahead log of exactly that input and nothing else, replaying the journal has to reproduce the run bit-for-bit. The replay proptest checks this by stepping the original and the replayed stream in lockstep and asserting equality *after every command*, not just at the end — so a divergence gets caught mid-round while the books and wallets are still full, not after settlement has wiped them. There's one subtlety I had to get right: the arena's physical layout isn't canonical (two logically identical books can differ byte-for-byte in which arena slots hold which orders), so replay is verified against a *normalized* snapshot capturing only logical state — resting orders in price-time order, positions, wallets, scoreboard — built from ordered `BTreeMap`s so the comparison itself is deterministic.

And the ring — the one piece of genuinely subtle concurrent code — is model-checked. Under `loom`, the acquire/release protocol between producer and consumer is checked across *all* possible thread interleavings on a tiny two-slot ring with three items, asserting the consumer sees exactly `[0, 1, 2]` with no loss and no reordering. `loom`'s state space explodes combinatorially, which is why the model is kept microscopic; a separate one-million-item real-thread stress test across several capacities is the probabilistic complement that catches what a tiny model can't reach.

<Callout type="lesson" title="To trust a clever data structure, diff it against a dumb one on every input">
The production book is fast because it's clever; the reference book is trustworthy because it's stupid. Running both on the same random stream and asserting equality after every command turns "is my intrusive-linked-list arena correct?" — a question I can't answer by staring at it — into "do these two disagree?", which a proptest answers in seconds. The reference book being slow is a feature: it shares no cleverness, so it shares no bugs.
</Callout>

The receipt is 517 of 517 tests passing under `cargo test --workspace --all-features`, with no pinned proptest regressions — the 600-case differential, the replay determinism check, the ring model, and the rest, all green. Set that against v1, where the cross-process money path was simply untested: the fast engine is checked event-for-event against an independent oracle on every input, and the old one took it on faith.

## How fast, and what actually binds it

The numbers, stated flat: **20,000 orders a second sustained for 25 seconds, in-system p50 of 2 microseconds and p99 of 66 microseconds, an engine loop averaging about 1.4 microseconds per iteration, and zero messages lost.** Pinning the rig, because these numbers are meaningless without it: two AWS `c7a.2xlarge` boxes (8 vCPU, 16 GiB each), a single market, a roster of 250 WebSocket clients. In-system latency is measured on the engine's own clock as `emit_ts − ingress_ts`, so it's immune to clock skew between machines.

The finding I actually care about isn't the throughput number — it's *where the bottleneck was*, and finding it meant taking the engine off the wire entirely. So I isolated it: ran the matching loop in-process, feeding it commands directly with no network, no egress, nothing but the engine and its rings, on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8488C (Sapphire Rapids, 8 vCPU, 15 GiB), thread pinned to one core. In isolation the engine processes a command in **p50 2.05 µs** (mean 1.27–1.42 µs across seeds) and sustains **630,000–690,000 commands a second**. That's the same ~2 µs scale as the wire run's engine clock — which means the 20k/s I measured on the wire wasn't the engine giving out. The engine was idling at a fraction of its capacity while something downstream throttled it. That used to be an inference from ring-depth telemetry; now it's measured directly.

Which lines up with the wire telemetry exactly. **The engine was never the bottleneck on its inbound side.** At every rate I drove the rig — nominally up to 150,000 orders a second — the depth of the inbound ring stayed at zero. The catch is that the engine was only ever *offered* about 37,000 commands a second, because egress throttled the pipeline upstream of it; so inbound-depth-zero says the engine kept up with everything it actually received, not that it chewed 150k. Between the in-process 630k+ and the wire ring-depth staying flat at zero, the two measurements agree: nothing on the ingress→match path was the wall.

Where it *does* stall is the output side. Each of the 250 WebSocket clients has a reliable delivery lane capped at 1,000 in-flight messages, and the engine publishes results into an outbound egress ring; when egress can't drain to the clients fast enough, that ring fills, and the engine **spins** on the full ring waiting for a slot (a `try_publish` that retries with a `spin_loop` hint). So the engine that "never blocks" does have exactly one thing it waits on — a full outbound ring — and that wait *is* the wall. Past roughly 25k/s the lanes overflow, the ring stays full, and every one of the 250 clients gets evicted at once. The backpressure is entirely on the output side; the read side of the test rig is what falls over, not the matching engine.

| offered/s | achieved/s | in-sys p99 | evictions |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2,000 | 1,793 | 16 µs | 0 |
| 10,000 | 8,975 | 16 µs | 0 |
| 25,000 | 22,455 | 2.1 ms | 0 |
| 50,000 | 29,567 | 4.2 ms | **250** |
| 150,000 | 28,572 | 2.1 ms | **250** |

The ladder reads cleanly if you know what you're looking at: microsecond latency and zero evictions through about 10k/s, a knee between 10k and 25k where p99 crosses from 16 µs into low milliseconds, and then a cliff at 50k+ where all 250 clients evict simultaneously — while the engine, again, keeps up on input. The knee is not the engine slowing down at matching. With ring-depth zero and a ~1.4 µs loop — now confirmed by the in-process 2 µs step — nothing on the ingress→match path produces 2.1 ms. It's the engine spinning on a *full outbound ring*: once egress can't drain to 250 clients fast enough, the publish side backs up, and the engine burns those milliseconds waiting for an egress slot before it can emit. So the 2.1 ms p99 is measured on the engine's clock but caused entirely downstream of it. This is a *system* ceiling imposed by egress and the single load box, and I want to be precise that it is not an engine matching ceiling.

Which brings me to what I can't yet claim. The design spec targets **at least 50,000 orders a second on a single engine core**, and I've measured that the engine *step* is fast enough — 630k+ in-process — but that is the engine in a vacuum, not the full wire path with real egress. The **≥50k/s single-core *wire* ceiling I still have not proven**: the egress/load-box limit binds first, so the one wire run I have can't see it *(not yet measured)*. There's a multi-box load generator built specifically to fix this — a coordinator and worker processes that drive disjoint ranges of competitors from separate machines and merge their latency histograms exactly — precisely so the read side stops being the constraint and the engine's real wire ceiling becomes visible. That run hasn't been recorded yet. One more trap I'll name so nobody else falls in it: the harness has a built-in `sweep` mode that reported 124,000 acked/s, and that number is *garbage* — a dedup-cache artifact where repeated client-order-ids after the first step were answered from cache without ever reaching the engine. I don't cite it and neither should you; the restart-per-rate ladder above is the trustworthy view.

<ThroughputWall />

So how much of the "70–130× over Python" is real? The defensible comparison is v1's *measured* saturation (150–286 accepted orders a second, first-hand) against v2's *measured* sustained rate (20,000, first-hand) — that gap is real, and it's where the 70–130× comes from. What I won't claim is a clean apples-to-apples: the two ran on different hardware, and any projection of what the *optimized* v1 could have done is exactly that, a projection. The number I'll stand behind is "the old one fell over in the low hundreds and the new one doesn't at 20k."

The last loop to close is the *why* behind each design choice — and this is the part I'd been carrying as "benchmark pending," so let me actually close it. Knowing the wire run couldn't see the engine's ceiling, I stopped trying to measure it through the network and started asking a different question in-process: of the choices that make v2's engine fast, *which one actually matters?* The way to answer that is to break each one on purpose. I built regressed variants — undo exactly one design decision, hold everything else fixed, replay an identical five-million-command stream — and measured the delta. The ablation replayed that one stream (a single seed) through each variant; every variant, run against the differential harness, produced a byte-identical **2,706,139 fills**, which confirms the *input* was identical across cells — though it's one seed, with no run-to-run variance bars. The ablation changed only speed, never the answer, which is exactly the guarantee the reference book buys.

The profiler kept pointing at allocation, so I started there. The clean way to isolate the arena is to hold book shape constant and flip only the allocator — the bottom two rows of the table below, both `BTreeMap`, one on the pooled arena and one going back to allocating each price level on the heap.

| variant | book shape | hot-path alloc | throughput | p50 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| shipped | flat array | pooled arena | 2,207,254/s | 512 ns | 2,048 ns |
| swap array → `BTreeMap` | `BTreeMap` | pooled arena | 1,973,341/s | 512 ns | 2,048 ns |
| also arena → heap | `BTreeMap` | per-level heap | 375,194/s | 1,024 ns | 131,072 ns |

Dropping the arena — those bottom two rows, book shape fixed at `BTreeMap` — cost **×5.3 in throughput** (1,973,341 → 375,194) and **×64 in tail latency**, p99 from 2 µs to 131 µs. That's the dominant lever by a wide margin, and it's the one the folklore doesn't lead with. The reason the tail specifically detonates: an allocator call can wander off into unbounded work — coalescing free lists, a syscall into the kernel for more memory — while handing out a pre-reserved pool slot is bounded, constant-time, every time. That's why removing the arena wrecks p99, not just the mean. Then I checked the famous trick, the array-vs-tree shape, with allocation held constant (the top two rows): the flat array bought **×1.12 throughput and identical p50/p99**. Real, worth keeping, and a small fraction of the allocation win — the arena mattered something like thirty times more. The shape is the thing the old exchange's `SortedDict`→bucket-array progression made me proud of; the measurement says the arena underneath it was doing most of the work.

One caveat on reading the latency columns: those histograms use power-of-two buckets (512 / 1,024 / 2,048 / 131,072 ns are exactly 2⁹ / 2¹⁰ / 2¹¹ / 2¹⁷), so p50 and p99 land on bucket edges and the clean ×2 and ×64 latency ratios are coarse — bucket-resolution artifacts, not precise measurements. Throughput is the precise signal here; latency is bucketed.

<AblationBars />

Then the journal, on a real xfs disk (the first time I ran this it landed in `/tmp`, which was tmpfs — RAM — so `fsync` was a no-op and the result was meaningless; the honest number needs a real spinning-or-SSD target):

| journal commit | throughput | p50 |
|---|---:|---:|
| group-commit (batch of 64) | 32,299/s | 0.26 µs |
| per-command `fsync` | 508/s | 2.10 ms |

Group-commit is **×63.5**. Per-command durability means a real 2.10 ms disk `fsync` on the path of every single order; batching amortizes it to nothing. That's the concrete cost behind "durability off the hot path" — it's not a figure of speech, it's 2 ms per order if you get it wrong.

One honesty note on what I *didn't* measure: I never isolated the lock-free ring against a mutex-guarded book as a throughput ablation. The single-writer/ring design is validated a different way — the inbound-ring depth staying at zero under load, plus the `loom` model check below — not by a regressed branch. So the measured trio is allocation, shape, and journal; the ring earns its place on correctness evidence, not a speed delta.

<Callout type="lesson" title="The biggest lever was the one I wasn't bragging about">
The array-shaped order book is the clever part, the part worth a diagram — and when I finally measured it, it was worth 12%. Not allocating on the hot path was worth 5.3× and a 64× better tail. The lesson isn't "shape doesn't matter"; it's that the win you can *see* in the data structure diagram is rarely the win that dominates the profile. I only know which is which because I broke each one on purpose and re-measured, instead of trusting the story I'd have told from the design.
</Callout>

## Competition day, and what I'd measure next

The original ran the actual event. 150 traders, real money, four live races, standings on the projector — and it held together, on a deploy path held up by a watchdog script and a cloudflared quick-tunnel that I would not describe as robust. That's worth saying plainly, because the rewrite is not a dunk on the thing that shipped. Shipping a working exchange for a room of 150 people on a five-week student deadline is the reason there was anything to rewrite. The v2 story only exists because v1 ran the race first.

The design decision I sat with longest is the risk model, because it's the one place engineering-correct and game-correct pull in different directions. v2's collateralized worst-case-loss model is more faithful to how a real prediction market works — you post what you could lose, you can't take a position you can't cover. But the competition ran on v1's model: a flat ±100 position limit, no collateral, bots exempt. And that difference changes how the game *feels*. Under a position limit, a new player can walk up and immediately take a swing to the cap without thinking about margin; under collateral, they have to understand reserve-and-settle before they can do anything — more correct, and less fun at a party. I went with collateral anyway, and the reason is the whole thesis of the rewrite in miniature: I'd rather the engine be the faithful thing and let the *event* dial the fun back in on top of it. The four-trait seam is exactly what makes that cheap — `RiskModel` is one impl, so a position-limit variant is a drop-in for whoever wants the party-game feel back, without touching the engine that earns the correctness guarantees. The shipped engine is collateralized; the seam is the hedge.

Here's the ledger of what's actually proven versus not:

| Dimension | v1 (Python / Redis) | v2 (Rust) |
|---|---|---|
| Topology | 3 units over a Redis bus, CQRS | 1 process, 1 machine, lock-free rings |
| Order book | `SortedDict` → 99-slot bucket array | flat array ladder + arena, O(1) hot path |
| Concurrency | 1 async actor/market (4 total), shared state | single writer, SPSC rings, busy-spin |
| Durability | none mid-round (heap is truth) | tee'd WAL, replay verified every command |
| Correctness | cross-process money path untested | 517/517, 600-case differential + replay |
| Risk model | ±100 position limit, bots exempt | collateralized worst-case loss |
| Measured perf | saturates ~150–286 RPS, p99 28–78 s | 20k/s wire sustained, p99 66 µs, 0 loss; 630k+ cmd/s in-process |
| What the design buys | — | arena vs heap ×5.3 tput / ×64 p99; array vs tree ×1.12; group-commit ×63.5 |
| Engine ceiling | — | ~2 µs/command; ≥50k/s single-core *wire* ceiling not yet measured |

Proven: 20k/s sustained with zero loss, 517/517 tests including the differential and replay determinism checks, the finding that the engine never bottlenecked on input, and — measured by breaking each choice on purpose — that no-hot-path-allocation is the dominant speed lever (×5.3), the array shape a real but secondary one (×1.12), and group-commit worth ×63.5 on real disk. Not yet proven: the ≥50k/s single-core *wire* ceiling, which needs the multi-box run to get egress out of the way. That one isn't done — it's the next experiment, and I'd rather list it as open than let it read as finished.

If there's one thing this whole rewrite argues, it's that the speed didn't come from Rust being faster than Python. It came from the *design* — and when I measured the design, the biggest single piece of it was the least glamorous one: never allocating while matching. The rest is a shape where correctness and speed turned out to be the *same* discipline: a single writer that's both race-free and lock-free, one sequencing point that's both fair and cheap, a journal that's durable precisely because it never touches the hot path, an arena that's fast precisely because it never touches the allocator, and an oracle that proves correctness on every command the engine will ever be fast enough to process. Going fast *was* going correct. The code will be public — clone it, run the same harness, and you'll get the same plots. And when the multi-box run finally tells me where the engine actually tops out on the wire, that's the next post.
