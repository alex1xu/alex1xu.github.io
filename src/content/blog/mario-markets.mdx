Mario Markets was a live prediction-market game for a Berkeley trading competition. About 150 players traded from their laptops while prerecorded Mario Kart races played on a projector. Each race had four tracked racers, so there were four related markets. The exchange was sized for roughly 250 connected players.

This post covers the Rust rewrite: what the engine owns, why it has one writer, how it is tested, and the data-structure choices on the matching path. The next post covers the separate problem of delivering its output to hundreds of clients.

## The market

Each market asks whether one racer will finish best of the four. A YES contract pays 100 cents if that racer wins and 0 otherwise. Traders buy or sell YES at integer prices from 1 to 99 cents. Buying YES at 40 cents therefore risks 40 cents for a possible 100-cent payout.

Each market uses a continuous limit order book. A limit order may wait in the book at its stated price; a market order takes available liquidity immediately. When a new order can trade with a resting order, the best price trades first. At the same price, the earlier resting order trades first. The engine also tracks positions, collateral, cancellations, settlement, and the round leaderboard.

## Why rewrite the first version

The event ran on a Python implementation. It was sufficient for the competition's actual load, but it saturated below 300 accepted orders per second in testing, with queued orders driving submit-to-fill p99 into tens of seconds. More importantly, state was split among async actors, Redis, caches, and Postgres. That made it difficult to answer a basic question: after an order, which component was authoritative for the book, position, and wallet?

The rewrite was not an attempt to make every part of the service low level. It was an attempt to put the economic state behind one owner and make its transitions reproducible.

## One engine owns the round

The Rust engine runs on one dedicated OS thread. It is the only code allowed to mutate the active round: every order book, position, wallet, collateral reservation, and settlement state.

<NewTopology />

Network I/O runs in Tokio, the standard Rust async runtime. Tokio tasks accept WebSocket traffic, manage connections, and pass commands to ingress. The engine is not a Tokio task. Matching is CPU-bound and latency-sensitive; it should not be scheduled alongside network tasks that repeatedly wait for sockets.

Ingress assigns each accepted command a global sequence number and an arrival timestamp. It sends the resulting `SequencedCommand` to the engine over a bounded single-producer/single-consumer ring. The engine consumes commands in that order and produces events into an outbound multicast ring.

This creates a useful boundary:

- The network side owns connections and I/O.
- The engine side owns the exchange state.
- The rings transfer messages between those owners without giving either side direct access to the other's state.

The inbound ring has exactly one producer, ingress, and one consumer, the engine. The outbound ring has one producer, the engine, and several consumers. Projection delivers reliable acknowledgements, fills, and cancels to the relevant client. Market data is separate and publishes current snapshots at 20 Hz; an old snapshot can be discarded because the next one replaces it.

### Why one writer

One writer means there is no lock around matching and no race between two threads changing the same book. It also makes the engine deterministic. Given the same ordered `SequencedCommand` stream—including command data, sequence numbers, and ingress timestamps—the engine produces the same events and logical state.

A single engine shard uses one core. The bare engine sustained about 630,000–690,000 commands per second in process, far above the load the event required. Several engine shards would require a partitioning rule and cross-market risk accounting.

### Journal and recovery

Ingress also tees each sequenced command to a journal writer. The journal records the engine's input stream, not a derived snapshot or output event. Replaying that stream reconstructs the round.

The journal is off the matching path. It group-commits disk writes rather than flushing every command. That gives a bounded durability window: a hard crash can lose recently acknowledged commands that have not yet been flushed. For a short live event where the fallback was restarting a round, that was acceptable. A slow disk could not delay trading; an admission-path bug that violated that rule is covered in part two.

## Matching and risk

For a new order, the engine validates the request, checks the user's risk limit, reserves worst-case collateral, and then matches against the best eligible resting orders. A buy takes the lowest available sells at or below its limit; a sell takes the highest available buys at or above its limit. A fill executes at the resting order's price. Any remaining good-till-cancel limit quantity joins the book; market and immediate-or-cancel remainders are released.

Collateral is the maximum amount a player could lose if the order fills. Buying `N` YES contracts at `p` cents reserves `N × p`; selling them reserves `N × (100 - p)`. The risk engine sits above all books so it can enforce a player's round-wide budget as well as a per-market position cap.

## Testing the fast book against a simple one

The production book uses cached best prices, compact order handles, and constant-time list updates. Those optimizations make it easy to introduce a bug that is almost invisible in hand-written examples. Instead of relying on more examples, I wrote a second book with a simple implementation.

<DifferentialHarness />

`RefBook` keeps resting orders in a flat vector and rescans it to find the next order to match. It is slow, but its behavior is easy to inspect. Tests feed both books the same randomized command stream and, after every command, compare emitted events and logical resting orders. They also check independent invariants on the production book: positions sum to zero within a market, collateral never goes negative, and a resting book is never crossed.

The same determinism supports replay tests: journal a generated command stream, read it back, and check that a fresh engine produces the same events and snapshot after each command. The rings are the concurrent part of the system, so their ordering protocol is checked separately with Loom, which explores small producer/consumer thread schedules exhaustively.

## Data layout on the matching path

The exchange used a fixed 1–99-cent price range. That mattered more than the nominal choice between a tree and an array.

<ArrayLadder />

A generic `BTreeMap` maps a price to a price level with a comparison-tree lookup. Here, a price is already a bounded integer, so the array ladder uses `index = price - low`. Each slot stores the head and tail of a FIFO queue for that price. Accessing a level is constant time; when the best level empties, finding the next one is a scan over at most 99 slots.

The array improved sustained throughput by about 1.12× over the `BTreeMap` comparison, with no material p99 change. That result was useful because it kept the claim proportional: the price range is so small that the tree has little work to do.

The larger effect came from order allocation. Resting orders live in a pre-sized arena. An insert takes a free slot; a complete fill or cancellation returns it. The queue links use compact arena indices rather than separately allocated list nodes. Against heap-allocated order nodes in the tree implementation, this raised throughput by about 5.3× and reduced p99 from 131 µs to 2 µs. The point was not that Rust normally requires manual allocation—it does not—but that allocation variability did not belong in the matching path.

## Where the first end-to-end ceiling was

At 20,000 offered orders per second with 250 clients, the service acknowledged about 18,000 orders per second with engine-side p99 of 33 µs. The bare engine was clearly not the limit. The output side was: one routing task had to distribute events across hundreds of client lanes.

Part two follows that bottleneck from roughly 20,000 to 97,000 end-to-end orders per second. The important distinction is that the matching engine was already fast; the remaining work was identifying where the rest of the system was serial, blocked, or measuring itself instead of the service.
