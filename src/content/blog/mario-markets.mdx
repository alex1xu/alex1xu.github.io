This spring, about 150 college students spent a weekend playing quantitative games at a competition run by Traders at Berkeley, a club I've been part of during my time at UC Berkeley. I was head of game development this year, and I led a new game called Mario Markets: competitors traded binary contracts on prerecorded Mario Kart races shown on a projector at the front of the room, each racer a separate market, all from their laptops. A few of us built the exchange and engine behind it over the months leading up to the event.

It was our first time building anything like this. I designed it for the event's load — about 150 people trading by hand, an estimated peak of maybe ten orders a second each — and under that load it worked, but not well. At the loudest moments of a race the latency got bad for some players: they'd fire an order and wait seconds to see it go through, or watch the board lag behind the action. It held the room, but "held the room" was hiding a lot. So I spent the months after rebuilding the whole thing from scratch — for the lessons, for an exchange with no lag next time, and for one I could extend with automated trading bots and a new game mode each year without redesigning the engine.

<Callout type="lesson" title="TL;DR">
The Python exchange (the first version, used at the competition) saturated somewhere south of **300 accepted orders a second**, and past that its submit-to-fill latency was pure *queueing delay* — orders piled up in a backlog and p99 climbed into the tens of seconds. The rewrite takes **20,000 orders a second** offered and acks about **18,000** of them at an engine-side p99 of **33 microseconds** — engine-side meaning ingress-to-emit on one clock, not the full round trip to a browser — while never dropping a message on a client's reliable lane.

That's a 60–70× gap. A few things I didn't expect going in:

- **Almost none of the speedup came from "Rust is faster than Python."** I benchmarked each of v2's design choices by breaking it on purpose and re-measuring. The dominant lever was **not allocating on the hot path** — not asking the OS for memory while matching an order — worth ×5.3 throughput and ×64 tail latency. The clever array-shaped order book everyone diagrams was worth ×1.12; batching the durability writes (group-commit) was worth ×63.5 on real disk. (An order book is the exchange's live list of resting buy and sell orders; more below.)
- **The rewrite had to get *more* correct, not less, to go fast.** The old system's matching core was fine the whole time. Everything that broke — lost orders, a double-settlement risk, race conditions — lived in the distributed plumbing wrapped around it. Going fast and staying correct pulled in the *same* direction here, not opposite ones, and most of this post is why.
- **A load test can lie to you about which side is slow.** Mine did, several times, and chasing the wrong ceiling cost me weeks. Later my *profiler* lied too, for a different reason.
</Callout>

## Context: Mario Markets

The instrument is a Kalshi-style binary prediction market: one market per racer, asking "does this racer win?" You trade YES or NO contracts priced in integer cents on a 1–99 ladder, a YES and a NO always sum to 100¢, and at the end of the race the market settles to 0 or 100 depending on who won. Buy YES at 40¢, your racer wins, you're paid 100.

Underneath, every market is a continuous limit order book with price-time priority: limit and market orders, partial fills, cancels, resting liquidity, a matching engine deciding who trades with whom and at what price. On top sit wallets, per-market P&L, and a cumulative leaderboard. Races run one at a time, and each race tracks four racers with a market on each — so four books are live at once and 150 people are hitting all of them.

That's the detail that turns throughput and latency from abstractions into something you watch fail in real time. Picture 150 laptops firing orders at the same four books the instant a blue shell knocks the leader out of first. A few hundred milliseconds of lag is the difference between a fill and a miss. The exchange doesn't get to be slow, and it *really* doesn't get to be wrong about who holds what.

## Version one: Python, Redis, and a board game underneath

The repo didn't begin life as an exchange. It started as a Snakes-and-Ladders board-game backend, and we grafted the exchange on top — which is why to this day the database tables carry a `calshi_` prefix, a fossil from the thing it used to be. That sets the tone: a system built fast, on a deadline, out of what was already there.

The topology was a React frontend, a FastAPI backend, a standalone Python asyncio matching engine, Redis doing double duty as both the command bus and the realtime fanout, and Postgres as the authoritative state.

<OldTopology />

We built it CQRS from the first commit, and aggressively async. `POST /orders` returned `202 Accepted` immediately — it did *not* persist or match the order. It dropped the command onto a Redis stream and returned. The engine consumed the stream, matched in memory, then persisted to Postgres and published fills over pub/sub; the browser learned what happened when a WebSocket message told it to invalidate its cache and refetch.

That's a clean pattern on paper, and it has a consequence you feel immediately: the UI's view of your P&L always lags a refetch. "I placed an order but I don't see it yet" isn't a bug in that architecture, it's the architecture — the acknowledgment and the truth live in different places and arrive at different times.

The matching *core* was fine — the whole surprise of the post-mortem hinges on it. One shared order book per market, a trade crosses when a YES bid and a NO bid sum to at least 100, FIFO price-time priority, fills at the resting order's price. That logic went in early and stayed sound the entire time. The engine at the center was never the problem. The trouble was all in the plumbing around it — the bus, the cache, the fanout, the places where two things had to agree and didn't.

### How v1 grew: six eras

We didn't design v1 and then build it. It grew, and the shape of the growth is the story. Six rough eras, from the commit log:

1. **A board game (late January).** Snakes and Ladders on a generic FastAPI "game" backend, with a market generator spinning binary markets off game state. The exchange core got grafted onto this chassis.
2. **The race pivot, and first deploy.** We dropped the live game for prerecorded Mario Kart races and stood the whole thing up on EC2 behind nginx. The order book was still a `SortedDict`.
3. **Optimize and add bots (mid-February).** Batch matching, a taking bot and a market-maker bot running inside the engine, `orjson`, smaller snapshots. This is where I started load-testing, and where the load-testing lie began.
4. **The balance rewrite.** We tore out a fragile `available + reserved` wallet model and replaced it with a single realized-P&L number. There's a commit literally titled `"SLIGHT BROKEN; rework of balance logic"`, which is exactly as reassuring as it sounds.
5. **The two-plane split.** The single biggest performance lever in v1's whole history: making the engine run *purely in memory during a round* and bulk-persist only at the end, which killed the per-fill database write on the hot path. In the same era we split the one FastAPI app into a control plane and a separate WebSocket gateway, because one process couldn't own both order entry and realtime fanout under load.
6. **Squeeze toward the target (final days).** The `SortedDict` → bucket-array order book (below), three Redis reads collapsed into one, `@dataclass(slots=True)`, three WebSocket gateways behind nginx `least_conn`, an auth cache, and the WebSocket race that became the sharpest scar (BUG-005, also below).

Every era added a layer around the matching core to make it faster or more scalable. And every layer added a seam — a place where two components now had to agree about state. Keep count, because that's where all the bugs turned out to live.

### The first real win: a tree, then an array

One v1 optimization is worth slowing down for, because the entire rewrite is built on the same insight.

I started the order book as a `SortedDict` — a balanced tree keyed by price, O(log n) inserts and lookups at each level. That's the reflexive data structure for a book, and for a general exchange with unbounded prices it's the right one. But this exchange doesn't have unbounded prices. Contracts trade on a 1–99 cent ladder. The set of possible price levels is exactly 99 integers, known ahead of time, forever.

So the tree is pure overhead. If a price level is just an integer in `[1, 99]`, then a level *is* an array index. I replaced the `SortedDict` with a fixed 99-slot bucket array: inserting or looking up a level became O(1) direct indexing, and finding the best price became a small bounded backward scan across at most 99 slots. No comparisons, no tree balancing, no pointer chasing.

<ArrayLadder />

I shipped this change but never benchmarked it in isolation on the old system — it landed in a batch of a dozen other optimizations in the final days. So the v1 speedup is **projected, not measured**: I know structurally that O(log n) level ops became O(1), and that a flat array of integers against a tree of boxed nodes isn't a close contest, but I don't have a clean before/after number from v1 to point at.

What I *do* have is the same idea, measured cleanly, because v2's order book is built on it — a flat array indexed by cent, this time with an intrusive linked list threading time priority through each level and an object-pool arena behind it so nothing allocates per order. When I later set out to find what actually made v2 fast, this array shape was the first thing I put on the scale. It surprised me: against a `BTreeMap` ladder with allocation held constant, the flat array bought a real but modest **~12% throughput** and *identical* tail latency. The clever part of the design was the smaller lever. I'll come back to the bigger one. But the seed of the whole rewrite is this one v1 observation: when your keys are 99 known integers, you don't need a comparison-based data structure at all.

## The wall: two ways a load test lied to me

The event lag was the symptom; I wanted the cause, so before the competition I load-tested v1 hard. The part I found most interesting, looking back, is that the load test I trusted to find the ceiling was lying to me about where it was — and telling the lie apart from the truth is the actual lesson.

I aimed for 1,500 aggregate orders a second: 150 users firing about 10 a second each, the load I'd designed for. What I got under that load was a *two-front* failure, and for a while I couldn't tell the two fronts apart.

**Front one: my load generator was the bottleneck, not the server.** I'd written the load client as a single Python process on one asyncio event loop — no `uvloop`, no multiprocessing. One process was opening 200-plus WebSocket connections, firing HTTP order submissions, *and* draining the inbound flood of book updates and trade notifications coming back, all on one core. Worse, I'd coupled the two: each simulated user awaited its own HTTP response before sending the next order. So when the server slowed down, my *client's* send rate collapsed right along with it — a closed loop that throttled itself to somewhere around 10–20% of target. It wasn't measuring the exchange's ceiling. It was measuring its own. I added a `--no-ws` flag to strip the inbound WebSocket load and measure the order path alone, and that's the point I realized the receiving side of my own test had been eating my throughput.

**Front two: underneath the throttled client, the server was also saturating.** Even at the sub-300 orders a second my crippled client could actually deliver, the engine couldn't keep up. One asyncio actor per market, four in all, topped out around **150–286 accepted orders a second**, with a submit-to-fill p99 between **28 and 78 seconds** and a backlog that climbed as high as **17,002** queued commands. I'd built a Grafana row called "Engine Queueing" to watch exactly this pileup, so it wasn't a surprise so much as a fear I'd instrumented and then confirmed.

<TwoFrontWall />

I *couldn't* cleanly measure the server's ceiling this way: my throttled client could offer ~150–300/s, and the server saturated at ~286/s, so the two walls sat almost on top of each other. Both were real, both pointed the same way — this design couldn't take the load I built it for. The network wasn't close to the limit (WebSocket traffic peaked around 3.7 MB/s), so this was compute and coordination overhead, not bandwidth.

<Callout type="lesson" title="A closed-loop load test on one event loop measures the load test">
When one process drives connections, sends orders, and drains responses on a single event loop, a saturated generator and a saturated server produce the *same* symptom: the achieved rate flatlines below target. I couldn't trust my server number until I'd ruled out my client — and with both ceilings sitting at nearly the same rate, I couldn't even do that; I just knew I was slow somewhere and had blinded myself to where. I learned to run the generator on separate hardware, and later on separate *machines*. That comes back at the end.
</Callout>

## The case for a rewrite: it was never just speed

Speed was the visible problem. The scarier one was quieter, and I only really saw it while writing the post-mortem: I had almost no way to prove the old system hadn't corrupted itself.

The durability story was the sharpest edge. The engine's `persist_*` functions — the ones that write state to Postgres — had *zero live callers* during a round. The in-memory heap was the only truth, and the database got written only at settlement. A crash mid-round didn't lose the last few seconds; it lost the entire round. And settlement had two divergent code paths, one reading in-memory state and one reading Postgres directly, with no shared lock — so if the two ever disagreed about what someone held, I could double-pay a position. They had to agree by luck, not by construction.

The rest of the ledger was of a piece:

- The Redis control bus was untyped and unacknowledged — commands went out as loosely-shaped messages with no delivery guarantee and no schema enforcing what a valid command even looked like. A dropped or reordered `settle_round` would just silently fail to settle.
- I had a per-endpoint flag that picked Redis-cache-as-truth or Postgres-as-truth. On a cold cache, one path returned zeros instead of falling back to the database — so "what's my balance" could quietly answer wrong.
- Concurrency was safe only by accident. The clearest case is **BUG-005**: once I sharded the WebSocket subscriber into multiple worker coroutines to scale fanout, they all read and wrote a shared per-channel sequence map with no lock, and raced. Sequence numbers clobbered each other and updates dropped or reordered. The fix — an `asyncio.Lock` — lives in one commit on a branch I named `ws-but-fixed-4real`. The branch name records how many tries it took.
- My docs had drifted from the code in ways that each hid a hazard: the position limit was hardcoded to 100, not the 200 the docs claimed; starting balances were 0, not the documented 100k; I never checked ownership on cancels, so nothing structurally stopped one user cancelling another's order.

Notice where every one of these lived: in the plumbing, never in the matching core. Each of the six eras bolted another layer around that core to make it faster or more scalable, and each layer landed on one of those seams — the bus, the cache, the sharded fanout — where correctness eventually leaked.

<Callout type="lesson" title="The bugs cluster at the seams optimizations add">
Six eras of optimization, six new boundaries where two components had to agree about state — and the matching core, the one part I never redistributed, was the one part that never broke. Speeding a system up by pulling it apart is the same move as manufacturing the seams it fails at.
</Callout>

So the brief I wrote for the rewrite wasn't "make it fast." It was "make it fast *and* provably correct, as one discipline instead of a tradeoff." One source of truth instead of a cache and a database that fork. One transport instead of a bus bolted onto a pub/sub. Real backpressure instead of an unbounded backlog. Invariants enforced by the type system instead of by a comment and a hope. One thing to head off an easy misread: money was integer cents in *both* versions. This was never a floats-are-wrong story. The argument for the rewrite is durability, typed transport, and the absence of double-settlement — not arithmetic.

## Version two: one thread, no locks, one source of truth

Every major v2 decision answers a specific v1 failure, so I'll walk them that way.

<V1V2SideBySide />

**v1 raced state across coroutines and processes; v2 has a single writer.** One engine OS thread owns every order book and every wallet for the live round. No locks on the hot path, no shared mutable hot-path state — one thread does all the mutation, so nothing the engine touches while matching needs a lock. The immediate payoff is determinism: feed the engine the same sequence of commands and you get bit-for-bit identical output, every time. That property is what the entire correctness story later rests on.

**v1's notion of "who was first" was ambiguous across a sharded bus; v2 decides fairness at exactly one point.** A single ingress task assigns the global sequence number to every command as it arrives, at one location in the code. That sequence number *is* the fairness anchor — the order the engine sees commands is fixed there and never reconsidered. Price-time priority means nothing if two components disagree about the time, and v1 had several clocks; v2 has exactly one that matters.

**v1 used Redis as both bus and fanout, which is where BUG-005 lived; v2 uses lock-free rings.** Commands flow from ingress to the engine over a single-producer single-consumer ring, and events flow back out over another. The engine busy-spins on a dedicated thread reading its inbound ring and never touches an async runtime — no `await`, no scheduler, no futures, just a thread in a loop. I'll come back to how I convinced myself the ring is actually correct, because a hand-rolled lock-free queue is exactly the kind of thing that's subtly wrong for months.

**And no order allocates on the hot path.** Every resting order lives in a pre-sized object-pool arena; matching relinks slots through the intrusive list instead of asking the allocator for memory. I'm flagging this as a first-class design decision, not an implementation detail, because when I later benchmarked what makes v2 fast, this turned out to be the dominant lever — bigger than the array shape, bigger than anything else I isolated. The number comes later; the commitment is here.

<HotPath />

**v1 had no mid-round durability; v2 journals before the engine ever sees a command.** Ingress sequences each command, then hands it to the journal *before* pushing it onto the engine's inbound ring. The ordering is deliberate: nothing reaches the engine that isn't already queued for durability, so the write-ahead log can never be missing a command the engine acted on. `fsync` is batched via group-commit — one flush per batch of 64, not one per command, on the journal's own thread, off the engine's hot path; when traffic goes quiet a 5 ms idle timer flushes whatever partial batch is waiting, so durability lag stays bounded even at a trickle. That batching isn't a small optimization: on a real disk it's the difference between roughly 500 and 32,000 commands a second, which I measure later.

That buys ordering, not durability-on-ack — the stronger thing it's easy to misread it as. A command is durable *in intent* — in the journal channel — before the engine matches it, but the actual disk `fsync` lands with the next group-commit up to a few milliseconds later. So the v2 acknowledgment is bounded-and-ordered durability, not "on disk the instant you're acked." It's the same *class* of tradeoff v1 had, except here the lag is bounded and the engine's input is journaled-in-intent first — which is the exact property that lets replay reconstruct a round.

**v1 had no collateral, just a ±100 position limit that bots skipped; v2 has one collateralized risk authority.** This is the one place the rewrite made a real *product* decision, not just an engineering one. v2 models risk the way a real exchange does: to hold a position you post collateral equal to your worst-case loss, and one shared authority reserves it at order entry — before the order can match, so a resting limit can never cross into a position its owner can't cover — settles against it on each fill, and releases it after. `reserve → match → settle → release`, with a per-market cap and a global budget. Real Kalshi is collateralized, so this is more faithful to the thing I was imitating. Whether it's the right call for a *party game* is a separate question I come back to at the end, because it changes how the game feels to play.

**And the whole thing is built to be extended without touching the hot path.** The instrument-specific logic lives behind four narrow traits — `Book`, `RiskModel`, `Settlement`, `Scoring` — so I could swap binary contracts for another instrument without going near the sequencer, the rings, or the journal. The test I hold an abstraction to is deletion: if I want to run a different kind of market next year, I write four trait impls and reuse everything else. That's a real seam, not a speculative one.

## Proving it didn't break: a second engine, on purpose

My fast order book had a trust problem: the cleverness that made it quick made it hard to believe. An intrusive linked list threaded through an object-pool arena, with cached best-price cursors and O(1) relinking, has a lot of moving parts a `SortedDict` doesn't — and every one is a place a subtle bug can hide and produce *almost* the right answer. I'd just spent a post-mortem learning that my last "it works on the day" system was quietly corruptible. I wasn't going to trust this one by reading it.

So I did what I should have done on v1: I raced the fast book against a slow one that's obviously right, on every input.

I wrote a second order book, `RefBook`, whose only design goal is to be correct in a way you can verify by reading it. Resting orders live in a flat `Vec`. To find the best order to match against, it rescans the *entire book* every single time and picks the best-priced, earliest one — O(n) per operation, embarrassingly slow, and impossible to get subtly wrong. It lives in its own crate and shares *no code* with the production ladder beyond the domain contract they both implement. That's the point. If the two shared a matching helper, a bug in that helper would corrupt both books identically and the comparison would pass while both were wrong. Sharing nothing means a bug in the fast book shows up as a *divergence* from the slow one.

The differential harness feeds an identical, randomly generated command stream to both books and, after *every single command*, asserts the two produced the exact same event stream and that their resting-order snapshots match.

<CodeCard file="differential.rs" lines="151–222">
```rust
let prod_ev = prod.apply(cmd.clone(), seq, ts, &mut prod_risk);
let ref_ev  = refb.apply(cmd,         seq, ts, &mut ref_risk);
prop_assert_eq!(prod_ev.as_slice(), ref_ev.as_slice(), "events diverged at step {}", i);
prop_assert_eq!(prod.resting_snapshot(), refb.resting_snapshot(), "book diverged at step {}", i);
```
</CodeCard>

<DifferentialHarness />

It runs 600 generated cases, and I made the command generator deliberately cruel: a tiny space of four users and eight client-order-ids, so crossing trades and self-trade-prevention fire constantly instead of almost never, roughly six orders per cancel, every mix of limit/market and GTC/IOC/FOK. On top of the event-equality check, each step asserts four cross-cutting invariants directly on the production book: contract and position conservation (net positions sum to zero per market — every contract someone is long, someone else is short), reserved collateral never goes negative, the book is never crossed at rest, and the production book's reserved collateral agrees with the reference book's. A bug that keeps the event streams equal but corrupts an invariant still gets caught.

Determinism gets the same treatment. Because the engine is a pure function of its sequenced input, and the journal is a write-ahead log of exactly that input and nothing else, replaying the journal has to reproduce the run bit-for-bit. The replay proptest steps the original and replayed streams in lockstep and asserts equality *after every command*, not just at the end — so a divergence gets caught mid-round while the books and wallets are still full, not after settlement has wiped them. One subtlety I had to get right: the arena's physical layout isn't canonical — two logically identical books can differ in which arena slots hold which orders — so I verify replay against a *normalized* snapshot that captures only logical state (resting orders in price-time order, positions, wallets, scoreboard), built from ordered maps so the comparison itself is deterministic.

And I model-check the ring — the one piece of genuinely subtle concurrent code. Under `loom`, it walks the acquire/release protocol between producer and consumer across *all* possible thread interleavings on a tiny two-slot ring, asserting the consumer sees every item with no loss and no reordering. `loom`'s state space explodes combinatorially, which is why the model stays microscopic; a separate one-million-item real-thread stress test is the probabilistic complement that catches what a tiny model can't reach.

<Callout type="lesson" title="To trust a clever data structure, diff it against a dumb one on every input">
I made `RefBook` slow on purpose. Running it against the fast book on the same random stream, asserting equality after every command, turns "is my intrusive-linked-list arena correct?" — a question I can't answer by staring at it — into "do these two disagree?", which a proptest answers in seconds. It shares no cleverness with the fast book, so it can't share a bug.
</Callout>

The receipt is **517 of 517 tests passing** under `cargo test --workspace --all-features` — the 600-case differential, the replay determinism check, the ring model, and the rest, all green. Set that against v1, where the cross-process money path was simply untested. The fast engine is checked event-for-event against an independent oracle on every input; the old one took it on faith.

## How fast, and what actually binds it

The numbers, stated flat: at **20,000 orders a second offered** to a roster of 250 clients, the engine acks about **18,000** of them at an in-system p50 of **2 microseconds** and p99 of **33 microseconds**, on an engine loop averaging about 1.4 microseconds per iteration, with zero reliable messages lost. (The ~10% between offered and acked isn't loss — it's cancels chasing orders that already filled, which reject as `unknown_order`; the metric counts acks, not the full offered stream.) The rig, because the numbers are meaningless without it: AWS `c7a.2xlarge` boxes (8 vCPU Intel Xeon 8488C, 16 GiB), a single market. In-system latency is measured on the engine's own clock as `emit_ts − ingress_ts`, so it's immune to clock skew between machines, and the load generator is fully open-loop — it fires each order on a fixed wall-clock schedule regardless of when replies come back, so a stall inflates the recorded tail instead of hiding behind a slower send rate. That's the property that makes the p99 trustworthy rather than a coordinated-omission mirage. One caveat I'll repeat when it matters below: my latency histograms use power-of-two buckets, so "p99 33µs" really means the 2¹⁵-nanosecond bucket — the tail lands *at or below* there, not exactly on it.

The throughput number bored me. What I chased was *where the bottleneck sat* — and finding that meant taking the engine off the wire entirely.

So I ran the matching loop in-process, feeding it commands directly with no network and no egress, thread-pinned to one core. In isolation the engine processes a command in **p50 2.05 µs** (mean 1.27–1.42 µs across seeds) and sustains **630,000–690,000 commands a second**. That's the same ~2 µs scale as the wire run's engine clock — which means the 20k I measured on the wire was not the engine giving out. The engine was idling at a fraction of its capacity while something downstream throttled it. That used to be an inference from ring-depth telemetry; now it's a direct measurement. (A separate, narrower micro-bench that exercises only the book's insert/match/cancel path, with no risk or journal, runs faster still — that's the 2.2M/s in the ablation table below. Different harness, different question; the 630–690k figure is the whole-engine-minus-the-wire number.)

The wire telemetry agrees. At every rate I drove the rig — nominally up to 150,000 orders a second — the inbound ring depth stayed at zero. The engine only ever *saw* about 26,000 commands a second (orders plus cancels), because past the saturation knee its slow clients get evicted and stop submitting — the egress wall throttles the engine's own input from downstream. But of everything it was handed, it never fell behind: inbound depth zero at every rate. Between the in-process 630k+ and the wire ring-depth pinned at zero, both measurements say the same thing: nothing on the ingress-to-match path was the wall.

<NewTopology />

The wall is on the output side, and I want to be exact about where, because I got this wrong in an earlier draft of my own mental model. The engine publishes each event into an outbound ring; a projection task drains that ring and fans every event out to the 250 per-client delivery lanes. Each reliable lane is capped, and when a client can't keep up, its lane fills — and rather than silently drop a message, the projection *evicts* that client. Past the knee, every one of the 250 clients overflows its lane and gets evicted at once. Here's the part that corrected me: I'd assumed the engine would end up spinning on a full *outbound ring*, waiting for the projection to drain it. It never does. Across the whole ladder, the engine's `outbound_full` counter stayed at **zero** while evictions hit **250**. The projection drains the ring faster than the engine fills it; the backup is entirely further downstream, at the per-client lanes. The engine that "never blocks" genuinely never blocked — not even on egress.

| 150,000 | 20,941 | 4.2 ms | **250** |

The ladder reads cleanly once you know what you're looking at: microsecond latency and zero evictions through 20k offered, then a cliff at 25k where all 250 clients evict at once and the client-observed p99 jumps into the low milliseconds. That jump is *not* the engine slowing down at matching. With ring depth zero, a ~1.4 µs loop, and `outbound_full` still at zero, nothing on the ingress-to-match path can produce milliseconds. It's the fan-out to 250 subscribers falling behind, the lanes overflowing, and the round-trip a client sees inflating while the engine underneath stays at ~16 µs — a system ceiling imposed by egress delivery, not by matching.

### I built a second rig to find the real ceiling, and was half right

The obvious objection to all of that: maybe 20k was just my single load box again — the v1 mistake, round two. My design spec targets at least **50,000 orders a second on one engine core**, and the in-process bench says the engine step clears that comfortably (630k+ is more than 12× the target) — but that's the engine in a vacuum, not the full wire path with real egress. I hadn't proven the wire ceiling, because on a single load box the egress-plus-load-box limit binds first and I couldn't see past it.

So I built the thing I'd wished for during the v1 post-mortem: a distributed load generator. A coordinator hands disjoint slices of the 250-client roster to worker processes on *separate machines*, each drives its slice, and the coordinator merges their latency histograms exactly (power-of-two buckets sum losslessly; quantiles get recomputed on the merged histogram, never averaged). Then I ran one engine box against three worker boxes, same 8-vCPU instance class, to take the load generator off the critical path once and for all.

I was half right, and the half I was wrong about is the interesting one. Three worker boxes did *not* move the plateau — it stayed right around 20,500 orders a second. So the wall was never my single load box. But it also wasn't the engine: across every rate up to 150,000 offered aggregate, the engine's `outbound_full` stayed at zero and its inbound ring depth stayed at zero, while `egress_evictions` pinned at 250. The bottleneck is the fan-out itself — delivering every fill and book update to 250 reliable subscribers fast enough — and it binds on the engine box's egress regardless of how many boxes push orders in. Three more senders couldn't help me; the constraint was how many *receivers* I had to keep in sync.

So I went to find out which part of the fan-out was the wall — and this is where I'd already made the performance engineer's cardinal mistake without noticing. I had a flame-graph-grade breakdown of *allocation*, the thing that turned out not to bind the wire, and nothing at all on egress, the thing that does. So I put `perf` on the engine box under a saturating 60k and looked at the per-thread CPU. Two threads were pegged at ~100%: the engine's busy-spin match core, which spins by design, and exactly one tokio worker. The other six workers sat between zero and a quarter. That one pegged worker is the projection: the single task that drains the outbound ring and routes each event to the right one of 250 per-client lanes. The per-client egress tasks — the ones that actually serialize JSON and write to sockets — were spread across the idle workers and were *not* the saturated resource. So the wall isn't the socket writes or the wire format; it's that one thread doing the routing, serially. The next lever has a name: shard the projection across cores. It's safe to now, over the rings — which is exactly the move that gave v1 BUG-005 when I tried it on a shared map without a lock.

<EgressProfile />

Then the profiler lied to me too, which I only caught because I'd been burned once already. Under `perf`, the saturation signature *changed*: `outbound_full` jumped to 1,157 and evictions dropped to zero — the opposite of the clean run. `perf`'s own overhead (it wrote 848 MB of stack samples in 15 seconds) had slowed the pegged projection worker enough that the engine's outbound ring backed up *before* any client lane overflowed. Same wall — the projection can't keep 250 lanes fed — surfacing at a different point because the measurement tool perturbed the thing it was measuring. I re-ran without `perf`, confirmed the pegged-worker picture held across three runs, and only then trusted it. A profiler is a load on the system too.

Which means the engine's true single-core wire ceiling is still a floor, not a number. It sustains at least ~26,000 commands a second through the full pipeline without ever backing up, and 630k+ in a vacuum, but the ≥50k/s *wire* figure remains unproven: the egress fan-out is a lower wall. The engine has never been the thing that saturates, on either rig — and I now know the next wall by name, and where in the code to go hit it.

One more trap, so nobody else falls in it. My harness also has a `sweep` mode that once reported 124,000 acked/s, and that number is *garbage*. Sweep runs its rate steps back-to-back without restarting the engine between them, and each load client restarts its order-id counter at 1 every step — so from the second step on, it replays client-order-ids the gateway has already seen, and the gateway answers them from its idempotency cache without ever touching the engine. The acked/s counter climbs beautifully while less and less real work happens. I don't cite it and neither should you; the restart-per-rate ladders above are the trustworthy view.

### So how much of "faster than Python" is real?

The defensible comparison is v1's *measured* saturation (150–286 accepted orders a second, first-hand from the old load-test artifacts) against v2's *measured* rate (~18k acked at 20k offered, first-hand). That gap is real, and it's about 60–70×. What I won't claim is a clean apples-to-apples: the two ran on different hardware, and any projection of what an *optimized* v1 could have done is exactly that, a projection. The number I'll stand behind is "the old one topped out in the low hundreds and the new one doesn't strain at 20k."

## Which design choice actually mattered: break each one and re-measure

I still owed myself the *why*. Knowing the wire couldn't see the engine's ceiling, I stopped trying to measure it through the network and asked a different question in-process: of the choices that make v2's engine fast, *which one actually matters?*

The way to answer that is to break each one on purpose. I built regressed variants — undo exactly one design decision, hold everything else fixed, replay an identical five-million-command stream — and measured the delta. Every variant, run through the differential harness, produced a byte-identical **2,706,139 fills**, which confirms the input was identical across cells. (It's one seed, so there are no run-to-run variance bars — the ablation changed only speed, never the answer, which is exactly the guarantee the reference book buys.)

The profiler kept pointing at allocation, so I started there. The clean way to isolate the arena is to hold the book shape constant and flip only the allocator — the bottom two rows below, both `BTreeMap`, one on the pooled arena and one going back to allocating each price level on the heap.

| variant | book shape | hot-path alloc | throughput | p50 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| shipped | flat array | pooled arena | 2,207,254/s | 512 ns | 2,048 ns |
| swap array → `BTreeMap` | `BTreeMap` | pooled arena | 1,973,341/s | 512 ns | 2,048 ns |
| also arena → heap | `BTreeMap` | per-level heap | 375,194/s | 1,024 ns | 131,072 ns |

Dropping the arena — those bottom two rows, book shape fixed — cost **×5.3 in throughput** (1,973,341 → 375,194) and **×64 in tail latency**, p99 from 2 µs to 131 µs. That's the dominant lever by a wide margin, and it's the one the folklore doesn't lead with. The reason the tail specifically detonates: an allocator call can wander off into unbounded work — coalescing free lists, a syscall into the kernel for more memory — while handing out a pre-reserved pool slot is bounded and constant-time every time. That's why removing the arena wrecks p99, not just the mean.

Then I checked the famous trick, the array-vs-tree shape, with allocation held constant — the top two rows. The flat array bought **×1.12 throughput and identical p50/p99**. Real, worth keeping, and a small fraction of the allocation win: a 5.3× lever against a 1.12× one. The shape is the part I was proud of on the old exchange, the part worth a diagram. The measurement says the arena underneath it was doing most of the work.

One caveat on reading the latency columns: these histograms use power-of-two buckets (512 / 1,024 / 2,048 / 131,072 ns are exactly 2⁹ / 2¹⁰ / 2¹¹ / 2¹⁷), so p50 and p99 land on bucket edges and the clean ×2 and ×64 latency ratios are coarse — bucket-resolution artifacts, not precise measurements. Throughput is the precise signal; latency is bucketed.

Then the journal, on a real xfs disk. (The first time I ran this it landed in `/tmp`, which was tmpfs — RAM — so `fsync` was a no-op and the result was meaningless. Only the real-disk number is worth citing, which cost me a re-run to notice.)

| journal commit | throughput | p50 |
|---|---:|---:|
| group-commit (batch of 64) | 32,299/s | 0.26 µs |
| per-command `fsync` | 508/s | 2.10 ms |

Group-commit is **×63.5**. Per-command durability means a real 2.10 ms disk `fsync` on the path of every single order; batching amortizes it to nothing. That's the concrete cost behind "durability off the hot path" — not a figure of speech, but 2 ms per order if you get it wrong.

<AblationBars />

One thing I *didn't* measure: I never isolated the lock-free ring against a mutex-guarded book as a throughput ablation. The single-writer ring design earns its place a different way — the inbound-ring depth staying at zero under load, plus the `loom` model check — not a regressed branch. So the measured trio is allocation, shape, and journal; the ring stands on correctness evidence, not a speed delta.

<Callout type="lesson" title="The biggest lever was the one I wasn't bragging about">
The array-shaped order book is the clever part, the part worth a diagram — and when I finally measured it, it was worth 12%. Not allocating on the hot path was worth 5.3× and a 64× better tail. I'd have bet on the shape from the design alone, and I'd have been wrong: breaking each choice and re-measuring is the only reason I know the arena underneath was the far bigger lever. Shape doesn't stop mattering — but the win you can *see* in the diagram is rarely the one that dominates the profile.
</Callout>

## Competition day

The original ran the actual event. 150 traders, four racer-markets a race, standings on the projector — and it held the room, on a deploy path propped up by a watchdog script and a cloudflared quick-tunnel I would not describe as robust. The rewrite is not a dunk on the thing that shipped. A few of us getting a working exchange in front of 150 people, for our first time building anything like it, is the reason there was anything worth rewriting. The v2 story only exists because v1 ran the race first.

The ledger of what's proven versus not:

| Dimension | v1 (Python / Redis) | v2 (Rust) |
|---|---|---|
| Topology | 4 processes over a Redis bus, CQRS | 1 process, 1 machine, lock-free rings |
| Order book | `SortedDict` → 99-slot bucket array | flat array ladder + arena, O(1) hot path |
| Concurrency | 1 async actor/market (4 total), shared state | single writer, SPSC rings, busy-spin |
| Durability | none mid-round (heap is truth) | journaled WAL, replay verified every command |
| Correctness | cross-process money path untested | 517/517, 600-case differential + replay |
| Risk model | ±100 position limit, bots exempt | collateralized worst-case loss |
| Measured perf | saturates ~150–286/s, p99 28–78 s | ~18k acked at 20k offered, p99 33 µs, 0 reliable loss; 630k+/s in-process |
| What the design buys | — | arena vs heap ×5.3 tput / ×64 p99; array vs tree ×1.12; group-commit ×63.5 |
| Engine ceiling | — | ~2 µs/command; wall is a single-threaded egress projection, not the engine |

Proven: ~18k acked at 20k offered with zero reliable loss; 517/517 tests including the differential and replay determinism checks; that the engine never bottlenecked on input on *either* rig; and — by breaking each choice on purpose — that not allocating on the hot path is the dominant speed lever (×5.3), the array shape a real but secondary one (×1.12), and group-commit worth ×63.5 on real disk. Not proven: the ≥50k/s single-core *wire* ceiling. The distributed run told me why I can't see it — the egress projection binds first — so the next experiment is to push the fan-out, not the engine.

## What I'd do next, and what I'd do differently

The profile handed me the next move for free: the egress projection is one thread doing all the ring-drain-and-route, pegged at 100% while seven cores idle. Sharding it — a projection per lane-group, each on its own core — is the obvious lift, and the ring architecture already makes it safe, which is the whole reason I can say that without flinching after BUG-005. That, plus the 4.6% of CPU I'm currently spending on tracing calls on the hot egress path, is where the next real throughput lives. Only then is the ≥50k/s wire ceiling actually measurable.

There's also a co-constraint I haven't ruled all the way out, and I'd rather name it than pretend it away: the journal's group-commit tops out around 32k commands a second on the disk I measured, only about 1.5× above the wire plateau. The wire runs never backed the journal up (inbound depth stayed at zero, which it wouldn't if the journal were stalling ingress), so it isn't the *current* wall — but on a slower disk it could become one before egress does, and a real "prove the 50k" run has to watch the journal thread as carefully as the projection.

Two things I'd do differently if I started over. First, I'd build the distributed load harness *first*, not after a single-box run sent me chasing a ceiling that was really my load generator — I made the v1 mistake's cousin a second time before I caught myself. Second, I'd have wired one honest end-to-end throughput number — acked, offered, and rejected, all three — into the harness from day one, instead of leaning on an acked-only rate that reads like loss until you know it's cancels. Both are the same lesson in different clothes: the measurement apparatus is part of the system, and it lies in exactly the ways you didn't instrument.

The bigger decision I'd make the same way is the risk model. v2's collateralized worst-case-loss model is more faithful to how a real prediction market works — you post what you could lose, you can't take a position you can't cover — but the competition ran on v1's flat ±100 position limit, and that difference changes how the game *feels*. A new player under a position limit walks up and swings to the cap without thinking about margin; under collateral, they have to understand reserve-and-settle before they can do anything. More correct, less fun at a party. I went with collateral anyway, and let the four-trait seam hold the escape hatch: `RiskModel` is one impl, so the party-game position-limit variant is a drop-in for whoever wants it, without touching the engine that earns the correctness guarantees.

If there's one thing this whole rewrite argues, it's that the speed didn't come from Rust being faster than Python. It came from the design — and the biggest single piece of it was the least glamorous one: never allocating while matching. The rest is a shape where the fast decision and the correct decision kept turning out to be the same decision. The single writer is race-free because one thread owns the state, and lock-free for the same reason. The journal is durable because it sits in front of the engine, and off the hot path for the same reason. It stopped surprising me somewhere around the third time.

The code is public — clone it, run the same harness, and you'll get the same plots (the single-box ones, at least; the exact multi-box numbers need four machines). And when I shard that projection and finally watch the engine top out on the wire instead of the fan-out, that's the next post.
